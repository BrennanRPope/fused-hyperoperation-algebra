What a Fermat counterexample would force, and where it breaks

Suppose ap + bp = cp for a prime p ≥ 5. Its Frey curve y² = x(x − ap)(x + bp) would have to satisfy fourteen properties at once. Ten are elementary and proved in Lean (one leans on a cited formula); four rest on deep theorems. Every odd prime falls out of the curve's level, the prime 2 never does, and level 2 has no room for anything. That collision is the whole proof.

Lead worth following? No. This is a clean map of a proof that is already complete, and nothing here points to new mathematics. Its use is as a lesson in method: the contradiction rests on exactly two facts (odd exponents divisible by p, and level 2 being empty), and every real curve we tried keeps at least one odd prime stuck in its level.

The hypothetical counterexample: conductor rad(abc). Every odd prime has exponent 2p·v in the discriminant, a multiple of p, so Ribet removes it. The prime 2 has exponent 2p·v − 8, never a multiple of p, so it stays. Level 2: the space of weight-2 cusp forms is empty (X₀(2) has genus 0).

A real curve, from 13 + 3⁵ = 2⁸ (conductor 78, minimal discriminant 2⁸·3¹⁰·13², checked in PARI). Mod 5, the prime 3 falls through (exponent 10), but 13 sticks (exponent 2), so the level lands on 26, where two cusp forms live. No contradiction, as it must be.

Where the properties collide

Rows 8 and 9 say the mod-p representation lives at level 2 with weight 2. Row 14 says nothing lives there. Three deep arrows connect them, and the Lean file states each as a field of one structure, DeepArrows, then proves squeeze: no counterexample survives, and flt_of_deep_arrows: Fermat for every n ≥ 3.

WilesThe curve is semistable (row 5), so it is modular (Wiles, Taylor–Wiles 1995). Without this there is no cusp form to lower at all.
MazurThe mod-p representation is irreducible for p ≥ 5, using the full 2-torsion (row 2). This is the arrow real curves escape through.
RibetIrreducible and modular, unramified at every odd bad prime (row 8): the level drops to 2 (row 9 stops it there). This is the arrow that consumes the p-th powers.

The escape Mazur closes

The curve from −125 + 128 = 3 has minimal discriminant 2⁶·3²·5⁶. Mod 3, the exponents at 2 and 5 are multiples of 3, so its level would collapse to 1 where nothing lives either. It survives only because it has a rational 3-isogeny (PARI finds degrees 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 in its class), so its mod-3 representation is reducible and Ribet's arrow does not apply. For the Frey curve with p ≥ 5 Mazur's theorem forbids exactly this.

Why 2 never leaves

In the normalised solution a ≡ 3 mod 4 and b is even, so the 2-part of the minimal discriminant is 2^(2p·v − 8) with v = v₂(b) (Lean disc_two_part). Since 2p·v − 8 ≡ −8 mod p and no odd prime divides 8, the exponent is never a multiple of p (two_exponent_not_multiple). So the level is 2, not 1. Either would do; 2 is what the arithmetic gives.

Mult-add's view

At each bad prime the curve's smooth points form the multiplicative group, whose chord law is mult-add (proved earlier, FreyPieces 3f). The Frey condition "exponent divisible by p" says Tate's parameter is a p-th power up to a unit, which in mult-add terms is a mult-add p-th power. That is Tate's uniformisation restated: clean, not new. No other step looks simpler on the rungs, and I haven't forced one.
