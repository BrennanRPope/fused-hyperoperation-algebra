The inverse of the Osgood test

For one equation the test works both ways. For systems and for Navier–Stokes it doesn't. If y' = f(y) has a finite Osgood integral, the solution blows up, and the blow-up time is exactly that integral. If a modulus ω has a finite integral at 0, the solution can leave 0 at any moment, so it isn't unique. With two or more components, or with an estimate instead of an equation, failing the test only means the proof stops.

On the mult-add ladder the verdict is sharp: every rung above 0 fails, rung 0 passes up to power 1, and right at that edge the test repeats itself one rung down, with the lift factor 1 + s used up at each step. And blow-up at infinity and leaving the wall −1 turn out to be one question, swapped by mult-add's own inverse −u/(1+u).

All the converses are classical; what's new is the mult-add packaging and the Lean forms. Nothing here is as promising as the soliton lead. Tags: proved here known, restated numerical check speculation.

Why the integral is a clock

Take y' = f(y) with f > 0, and measure height with G(y) = ∫y₀y ds/f(s). Along any solution, d/dt G(y(t)) = y'/f(y) = 1. So in the coordinate G the motion is uniform: the solution walks down a straight road at unit speed, and G(y) is the time it takes to reach height y. known, restated

That is the whole geometric underpinning. The Osgood test asks one question: is the road finite? If the road to infinity has finite length T, the walker gets there at time T, which is blow-up. If the road out of 0 has finite length, the walker can step onto it at any moment, which is non-uniqueness. If both roads are infinite, nothing happens in finite time.

In mult-add terms the clock for y' = 1 + y is log(1 + y), mult-add's logarithm: the rung-1 law runs at unit speed on the log scale and the road never ends. For y' = (1+y)² the clock is 1 − 1/(1+y), the reciprocal of the lift, and the road has length exactly 1.

Converse 1: a finite clock forces blow-up, at exactly that time

Theorem. Let f be continuous and positive on [y₀, ∞). The solution of y' = f(y), y(0) = y₀, exists exactly for 0 ≤ t < T*, where T* = ∫y₀∞ ds/f(s). If T* is finite, y(t) → ∞ as t → T*. known, restated

Proof. Since y' = f(y) > 0, y is increasing and G(y(t)) = t. G is strictly increasing, so y(t) = G⁻¹(t), defined for exactly the times G takes, [0, G(∞)). Nothing else can happen. ∎

The examples in the figure check this numerically: y' = (1+y)² blows up at 1, (1+y)1.5 at 2, (1+y)³ at ½, each matching its integral to nine digits. numerical check

The comparison form. If you only know an inequality, the direction matters. y' ≥ f(y) with a finite integral forces blow-up by time T* (Bihari–LaSalle in reverse). But y' ≤ f(y) with a finite integral forces nothing: y' = 0 satisfies y' ≤ y². known, restated This is exactly the Navier–Stokes situation: the enstrophy estimates are upper bounds.

Lean: the climb hits the wall

The continuous solution t/(1−t) of y' = (1+y)² obeys an exact difference law with no error term: y(t+h) − y(t) = h·(1 + y(t))(1 + y(t+h)), which is h times the lift of y(t) ⊕ y(t+h). The discrete climb with that law runs on a straight clock: 1/(1+yₙ) = 1/(1+y₀) − n·h. So started at y₀ = 0 with step h = 1/m, it cannot take m finite steps: at step m the clock reads 0. The rung-1 climb yₙ₊₁ = yₙ ⊕ h has 1 + yₙ = (1+y₀)(1+h)ⁿ and never reaches the wall. proved here (Lean, uncompiled)

Converse 2: a finite clock out of 0 forces non-uniqueness

Theorem. Let ω be continuous, nondecreasing, ω(0) = 0, ω > 0 on (0, δ], and subadditive (true for every concave modulus). If Ω(r) = ∫0r ds/ω(s) is finite, then x' = ω(max(x, 0)), x(0) = 0 has a right-hand side with modulus ω and infinitely many solutions: x ≡ 0 and, for every c ≥ 0, the curve that sits at 0 until c and then follows Ω⁻¹(t − c). If Ω is infinite, Osgood's theorem says every equation with modulus ω has unique solutions. So for concave moduli, Osgood's condition is exactly uniqueness. known, restated

Proof. Subadditivity gives |ω(a⁺) − ω(b⁺)| ≤ ω(|a⁺ − b⁺|) ≤ ω(|a − b|). Ω is increasing with Ω' = 1/ω on (0, δ], so x = Ω⁻¹(t) has x' = ω(x) for t > 0. At t = 0, Ω(x) ≥ x/ω(x) because ω ≤ ω(x) on [0, x], so x(t)/t = x/Ω(x) ≤ ω(x) → 0 = ω(0). Shifting by c is allowed because the equation doesn't depend on t. ∎

The same holds in a far stronger class: Colombo and Kumar (2026) build, for every non-Osgood modulus, a divergence-free velocity field with that modulus whose flow is not unique on a set of positive measure (arXiv 2601.12096). known, restated On the passing side, Yudovich's uniqueness theorem for 2D Euler with bounded vorticity runs on the modulus r·log(1/r), whose clock out of 0 is log log(1/r), infinite but barely.

Lean: the fan and the Lipschitz contrast

On the integer grid the trapezoid scheme of x' = 2√x is x(n+1) = x(n) + √x(n) + √x(n+1). For every start time c, xc(n) = (n − c)² (zero until c) solves it with √xc = n − c, every one starts at 0, and different c give different solutions. The trapezoid scheme of the Lipschitz x' = x started at 0 has only the zero solution. proved here (Lean, uncompiled)

The mirror: leaving the wall is blow-up seen through the inverse

Put s = 1/x. If x' = ω(x), then s' = −s²·ω(1/s). So a solution leaving 0 in finite time is a solution arriving from infinity in finite time, which is blow-up run backwards, and the two clocks are the same integral: ∫0δ dr/ω(r) = ∫1/δ∞ ds/(s²ω(1/s)). known, restated The modulus √r mirrors to the growth s3/2; r·log(1/r) mirrors to s·log s.

In mult-add, the mirror is the inverse. Under the lift, mult-add's inverse −u/(1+u) is the reciprocal: 1 + (−u/(1+u)) = 1/(1+u). It sends u → ∞ to the wall −1. A Riccati step uₙ₊₁ − uₙ = h(1+uₙ)(1+uₙ₊₁) becomes, through the inverse, a step of exactly −h: the climb to infinity is a walk at constant speed into the wall. So "does it blow up?" at infinity and "can it leave?" at the wall are one question. proved here (Lean, uncompiled)

Where the inverse fails

Systems. The test reads only the size of the vector field, and size doesn't decide. The rotation field |x|²·Jx and the radial field |x|²·x both have speed r³. The first is perpendicular to x everywhere, so d/dt |x|² = 0 and nothing ever grows. The second pushes straight out, so r² obeys the Riccati law (r²)' = 2(r²)² and blows up at 1/(2r₀²). proved here (Lean, uncompiled) numerical check Navier–Stokes is the first kind on paper: its nonlinearity is perpendicular to the velocity in the energy sense (u·(u·∇)u integrates to 0), the same cancellation as the dyadic energy identity on the last page.

Navier–Stokes. The 3D enstrophy bound is an upper bound that fails Osgood, so it gives local smoothness and stops. Tao (2016) showed the gap can't be closed by size estimates alone: an averaged Navier–Stokes with the same energy identity and the same harmonic-analysis bounds does blow up in finite time (J. Amer. Math. Soc. 29, 601–674). So failing the test is consistent with blow-up and with smoothness, and the real equation stays open. known, restated

When a lower bound does match. The 2D Euler vorticity gradient obeys an upper bound of the form y' ≤ C·y·log y, which is the second rung of the ladder below and gives double-exponential growth, E² in mult-add terms. Kiselev and Šverák (2014) built data in a disk that actually grows double-exponentially, so that bound is sharp (Annals 180, 1205–1220). That's the inverse holding, but by a separate construction, not by the test. known, restated

On the rung ladder

The test moves down one rung exactly. Write the growth law as f(s) = (1 + s)·g(s) and substitute s = E(x), so 1 + s = eˣ and ds = (1 + s)dx. Then ∫ ds/((1+s)g(s)) = ∫ dx/g(E(x)). The lift factor 1 + s is used up, and what's left is the same test for g read one rung down. proved here (on paper; discrete form in Lean)

In Lean the discrete form is Cauchy condensation. The blocks [2ʲ, 2ʲ⁺¹) start at 1 + 1⊕j (the mult-add lattice of 1) and have length 2ʲ, the lift factor. The partial sums are squeezed by Σ 2ʲ·a(2ʲ) both ways, so the series passes iff its condensed series passes. One rung down, 1/k becomes all ones (so 1 + ½ + … + 1/(2ⁿ − 1) ≥ n/2) and 1/k² becomes a geometric series (so its sums stay below 2). proved here (Lean, uncompiled)

The ladder of laws that pass

Apply the shift again and again. With L = log(1 + ·), the laws f₁ = 1 + y, f₂ = (1+y)(1+Ly), f₃ = f₂·(1 + L²y), … have clocks Ly, L²y, L³y, all infinite, and their solutions are exactly the rungs: E(t), E²(t), E³(t). known, restated numerical check At every level the edge is power 1 on the last factor: f₂·(1+Ly)0.3 already fails, with clock 1/0.3.

There is no last law that passes. The ladder f₁, f₂, f₃, … never reaches an edge: for any law that passes there is a faster one that still passes (du Bois-Reymond, Hadamard). known, restated One neat fact about where the ladder heads: the vector field V that generates the fractional rungs (V = d/dt Et at t = 0) obeys V(E(x)) = (1 + E(x))·V(x), its clock from x to E(x) is exactly 1, and it outgrows f₂ while still passing. So the rung flow itself runs just past every finite level of the ladder. This is the known behaviour of the super-logarithm's derivative, not a new result. numerical check known, restated

One gap in rigour: "every fractional rung t > 0 beats every power of x" uses V(y)/y increasing for the regular iteration. I checked that numerically up to y = 10¹⁰⁰, not by proof.
