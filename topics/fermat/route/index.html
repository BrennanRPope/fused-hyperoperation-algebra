The route in circle-operation geometry: the proof is easy exactly where the curve is the circle operation

  1. Fermat is a circle power against an ordinary one. Divide aᵖ + bᵖ = cᵖ by aᵖ and set t = b/a, s = c/a − 1. The equation becomes tᵖ = [p]s, where [p]s = s ∘₁ s ∘₁ … ∘₁ s = (1 + s)ᵖ − 1. proved
  2. Reduce to primes. Every n ≥ 3 is a multiple of 4 or of an odd prime, so exponents 3, 4 and the primes from 5 up are enough. Exponents 3 and 4 are proved here in full, 3 by building the Eisenstein integers from scratch and 4 by Fermat's own descent. proved
  3. Build the Frey curve y² = x(x − aᵖ)(x + bᵖ) from a solution, after normalizing so a ≡ 3 mod 4, b is even and gcd(a, b) = 1. Its three half-way points are 0, aᵖ and −bᵖ. Its discriminant is 16(abc)²ᵖ, and its minimal model has 256·Δ = (abc)²ᵖ: a perfect p-th power. proved
  4. At every bad prime the curve pinches into the circle-operation node, never a cusp. No prime divides both Δ and c₄ = A² + AB + B². At the node, three points lie on one line exactly when their coordinates combine by the circle operation to zero. This is semistability, and it is the circle operation's own shape. proved
  5. Near each pinch the curve is a circle-operation cylinder glued into a torus (Tate's curve, u ~ q·u). The gluing size q has a p-th power exponent, so the p-torsion points line up without anything new at that prime: the bad primes become invisible to E[p] (Hellegouarch, Frey, Serre). known
  6. At the good primes the curve is not the circle operation. Frobenius turns instead of stretching (ap² < 4p), the turning country found in the elliptic work. Wiles and Taylor–Wiles prove the curve is modular here, with Langlands–Tunnell, the 3–5 switch and R = T. Mazur supplies irreducibility. out of reach
  7. Ribet strips the invisible primes from the level, leaving 2. The modular curve X₀(2) is a sphere, genus 0, so it carries no weight-2 cusp form to match. Contradiction. The genus count is proved here; Ribet is not. out of reach

A bad prime pinches the loop shut into a node

The Frey curve's oval runs from −B to 0 and its open branch starts at A. When a prime divides A, the curve seen through that prime has A = 0: the oval and the branch touch at the origin and make a double point. The two tangent lines there are the node's walls.

The Lean piece frey_semistable says this is all that ever happens: c₄ never shares a prime with Δ, so the pinch is always a node with two distinct walls, never a cusp where the walls merge. frey_reduces_to_node shows the reduced curve is exactly the node family whose chord law the library already ties to the circle operation.

On the node, three points on a line multiply to 1

Take the pinched curve y² = x²(x + 1). Every line through the node at slope t meets the curve once more, at x = t² − 1. Give that point the coordinate s = −2/(t + 1), so its lift is 1 + s = (t − 1)/(t + 1).

Three points are collinear exactly when s₁ ∘₁ s₂ ∘₁ s₃ = 0, which under the lift says (1 + s₁)(1 + s₂)(1 + s₃) = 1. The drawing uses t = 1.3 and t = 1.7; the third point is forced to the lift that makes the product 1. Proved in general as node_chord_circ.

Why the bad primes drop out: the glued cylinder lines up when q is a p-th power

Take the circle operation's lifted line, the punctured plane under multiplication, and draw it in log coordinates: angle across, log-size up. That is a cylinder. Tate glued its top to its bottom (u ~ q·u) to make the curve near a bad prime into a torus.

The p-torsion points are the u with uᵖ a power of q. Here p = 5 and q = t⁵, so all 25 points sit on a clean grid built from t and the fifth roots of 1, and nothing new has to be adjoined at that prime. The piece that makes q a p-th power is frey_disc_valuation: p divides the exponent of every odd prime in Δ. The step from there to "unramified" is known math, stated here and not formalized.

The capstone, word for word:

def NoFreyCurve : Prop :=
  ∀ (p : Nat), IsPrime p → 5 ≤ p → ∀ a b c : Int,
    Int.gcd a b = 1 → a * b * c ≠ 0 → a % 4 = 3 → b % 2 = 0 → (32 : Int) ∣ b ^ p →
    a ^ p + b ^ p ≠ c ^ p

theorem flt_of_no_frey_curve (H : NoFreyCurve) : ∀ n, 3 ≤ n → FLT n

Known versus new

Known: every mathematical statement here. Exponent 4 is Fermat's, exponent 3 is Euler's and Gauss's, the Frey curve facts are Frey, Serre and Ribet's, and the Tate curve is Tate's. Exponents 3 and 4 have been formalized before, in mathlib.

New here: these proofs written from scratch in core Lean on the circle-operation library, and one geometric reading that holds the route together: the half of Wiles's proof that is easy is the half where the curve is the circle operation (the pinched bad primes), and the hard half is where it turns instead (the good primes). That reading restates known facts; it is not a new theorem.

No new lead as strong as the solitons came out of this. Fermat's Last Theorem itself is not proved here: the 13 open pieces carry nearly all of its difficulty.
