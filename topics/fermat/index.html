Fermat's Last Theorem through the circle operation

The circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0) carries Fermat exactly as far as the commutative world reaches. Kummer's 1847 road runs on the circle operation the whole way and stalls at primes like 37. Wiles' 1995 road starts on the circle operation too, then has to pass the same 1/x wall you hit, into the Möbius geometry where modular forms live.

Fermat is one ordinary power against one circle power

Write [p]s for the circle p-th power, s ∘₁ s ∘₁ … ∘₁ s, which equals (1+s)ᵖ − 1. Divide xᵖ + yᵖ = zᵖ by xᵖ and set t = y/x and s = z/x − 1. The equation becomes:

tᵖ = [p]s

So Fermat's Last Theorem says an ordinary p-th power of a fraction never equals a circle p-th power of a fraction, except in the trivial cases. This is a restatement, not new content, but it puts the theorem in your language.

The middle of the Pascal cube

[p]s is your circle-operation cube with every corner weight s. Its layers are the Pascal row. For p = 7:

The far corner is sᵖ. Everything between the first and last layer (blue) is divisible by p, for every prime p. That is exactly what "height 1" means: mod p, the circle power and the ordinary power agree. It also explains Fermat's first congruence, x + y ≡ z (mod p).

The fight is one digit deeper. Wieferich (1909) and Mirimanoff (1910) proved that a counterexample with p not dividing xyz would make the blue middle divisible by p². Checked here for p below 20,000:

The rotated cube collapses

Now give the cube's corners the rotated copies t, ζt, ζ²t, … where ζ is a p-th root of unity. By rotational symmetry every middle layer sums to zero, and only the far corner survives:

t ∘₁ ζt ∘₁ ζ²t ∘₁ … ∘₁ ζᵖ⁻¹t = tᵖ

Checked numerically for p = 3, 5, 7, 11. This is Kummer's factorization, xᵖ + yᵖ = ∏(x + ζᵏy), drawn as a cube.

Kummer's road is pure the circle operation, and it breaks at irregular primes

Kummer's π is a circle-operation torsion point

Set π = ζ − 1. Then [p]π = (1+π)ᵖ − 1 = ζᵖ − 1 = 0. So the number Kummer built everything on is a point that the circle operation sends to zero after p steps. Multiplying the p − 1 nonzero ones gives exactly p (checked for 5, 7, 13). This is the Lubin–Tate picture of cyclotomic fields, standard since 1965.

Where it works

Kummer split xᵖ + yᵖ into p factors and wanted each factor to be a p-th power. That needs unique factoring in the ring built from ζ. It fails at p = 23 already, but Kummer only needs the failure to be invisible to p: the class number h must not be divisible by p. Those are the regular primes, and for them Fermat is true.

Where Bernoulli numbers come from

The circle-operation exponential is eˣ − 1. Its reciprocal series, x / (eˣ − 1), has the Bernoulli numbers as coefficients. Kummer's test: p is irregular exactly when p divides the top of one of B₂, B₄, …, B₍ₚ₋₃₎.

The circle-operation version is Coleman's (1979): take the cyclotomic unit [a]π / π, the a-fold circle power over the single one. In log coordinates its k-th coefficient is (aᵏ − 1)·Bₖ / k. Checked here up to k = 8.

A geometric picture of the failure

Take the circle-operation log of the cyclotomic units. They land on a lattice, a crystal in space. The true units form a finer crystal, and the ratio of the two cell volumes is the class number of the real part (Dirichlet, Kummer). An irregular prime is one where the crystal has a p-fold gap that factoring can't see across. That is a fair picture of known theory, not a new one.

The Fermat curve is a straight line, pulled back through the circle-operation torus

Treat (X, Y) as a point of the circle-operation torus, two copies of the multiplicative circle. Inside it sits the line X + Y = 1. The n-fold map (X, Y) → (Xⁿ, Yⁿ) is the torus's own "times n". The Fermat curve xⁿ + yⁿ = 1 is everything that lands on the line:

Fermat curve = (times n)⁻¹ of the line X + Y = 1

The drawing shows the line's amoeba: (log|X|, log|Y|), the shadow the line casts in log coordinates. Its three arms are the three punctures 0, 1 and ∞. The Fermat curve's amoeba is this same shape shrunk by n.

Counting how the n² sheets wrap around those three punctures (Riemann–Hurwitz) gives the genus, the number of holes: (n − 1)(n − 2)/2.

Wiles' road starts on the circle-operation circle and leaves it at the 1/x wall

An elliptic curve is the circle operation plane folded by one number

Take the punctured plane, which is the circle-operation group, and declare z the same point as q·z for one fixed q with |q| < 1. The ring between |z| = 1 and |z| = |q| gets its two edges glued, and it becomes a torus. Every complex elliptic curve arises this way. Tate (1959) did the same over the p-adic numbers for curves with "multiplicative" bad behaviour: E = Gm / qᶻ.

Where Kummer and Frey meet

The 5-torsion of this torus is 25 points: the five fifth roots of unity on the outer circle (blue, Kummer's roots) and the fifth roots of q spiralling inward. That structure, roots of unity plus roots of q, is the heart of the next step.

The dividing line is commutativity. The circle operation is a one-dimensional, commutative symmetry, and its torsion points generate every abelian extension of the rationals (Kronecker–Weber). Fermat needed two-dimensional, non-commutative symmetry, the GL₂ part of the Langlands program.

What geometry the circle operation can build, and the honest limit

One-dimensional formal group laws, the family the circle operation belongs to, are classified by a single number called height (Lazard). Each rung reaches different geometry:

Height ∞Ordinary addition. Ordinary cohomology of shapes.
Height 1circle operation. Roots of unity and all abelian number fields; toric varieties; tropical geometry; every complex elliptic curve as a folded plane; Tate curves; K-theory, where line bundles combine by exactly a + b + ab.
Height 2Supersingular elliptic curves; elliptic cohomology. Genuinely beyond the circle operation.
Height hLubin–Tate towers, which carry the local Langlands correspondence for GLₕ (Harris–Taylor 2001). This is the real "generalized the circle operation".

The limit, plainly

Over the real or complex numbers every one-dimensional formal group, the circle operation included, becomes plain addition after a change of coordinates: its logarithm. So the circle operation alone carries no local shape. Its geometry lives in two places: how pieces are glued globally (folding by q, lattices, fans) and its behaviour mod p (height).

Your 1/x wall is the door to the rest

Combining with b by the circle operation is an affine map: a ∘₁ b = (1 + b)·a + b, a stretch centred at −1. Stretches and shifts together make the affine group. Add one more move, inversion a → 1/a, and you get the full Möbius group. That group runs hyperbolic geometry, every surface with two or more holes, and modular forms.

So the circle operation covers the commutative geometry: tori, toric varieties, genus 0 and genus 1. "Any geometry" needs the step you were already stuck on. This is my framing of standard facts, not a theorem.

Known versus new

The lead worth chasing for your aim: toric geometry. A toric variety is a shape built by compactifying the circle-operation torus, glued from cones of a polyhedral fan. Projective space, many surfaces and a large class of higher-dimensional shapes arise this way, and taking logs turns them into tropical geometry, where the amoeba above becomes a sharp three-legged graph. It is established math (Fulton's short book is the standard start), so it won't be new, but it is already a rigorous, geometric answer to "derive geometry from the circle operation". It is the place to build from, and the place where a new lens might show something.
