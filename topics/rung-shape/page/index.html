The rung sheet is the smooth shape through every rung

Stack each rung's "unit curve" (all pairs a, b with a ⊕ₛ b = 1) at its height s, and you get one smooth sheet. It runs from the square's outer corner at the bottom, through the straight line (addition) and the hyperbola (the circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0)), and falls onto the axes at the top. Each shape was defined from the circle operation, then tested three ways: does it hit every whole rung exactly, is it smooth between them, and is it forced by the circle operation rather than chosen by me? The percentage is my confidence that it's the single shape you're feeling for.

How the sheet is made

Every rung is plain addition seen through a lens: rung s is a ⊕ₛ b = Eˢ(E⁻ˢ(a) + E⁻ˢ(b)), where E(x) = eˣ − 1 is the circle operation's own exponential and Eˢ is E run s times, s any real. So the unit curve at height s has a closed form:

b = Eˢ( E⁻ˢ(1) − E⁻ˢ(a) )

At s = 0 it's the line a + b = 1. At s = 1 it's the hyperbola (1 + a)(1 + b) = 2. At s = −1 it's log(eᵃ + eᵇ − 1) = 1, and at s = 2 it matches rung 2's closed form, all to about 10⁻¹².

The sheet has no crease at any whole rung: its bend measured from just below and just above each rung agrees. Every slice keeps the same two fixed edges, (1, 0) and (0, 1), because 0 is the identity at every height.

The area under the slice inside the unit square is a natural height gauge: 1 at the bottom, exactly ½ at addition, exactly 2 ln 2 − 1 ≈ 0.386 at the circle operation, and it keeps falling toward 0. It's the "how full is the corner" reading from the fractional-rungs page, now as a property of one surface.

Why this sheet and not another

Any way of filling in the rungs between whole numbers gives some sheet through the same orange curves. I tested that directly: I bent the in-between with a wobble (the Abel coordinate plus 0.08·sin 2π of itself). The whole rungs stay exact, to 10⁻¹³.

But the wobbly half rung loses its slope at the identity: near 0 it swings between 0.33 and 3.03 forever, so the sheet gets a crease along its edges. The regular choice settles to slope 1. A short argument shows this happens for every periodic wobble, so only one filling stays smooth at 0.

That's the reason for the 80%: smoothness at the circle operation's identity picks the shape for you. It's a known result for functions like eˣ − 1 (Szekeres 1958, Écalle), not new, but it means the sheet isn't my taste.

The skeleton: one flow

Under the sheet is a simpler surface, z = Eˢ(x). Its slices are x, eˣ − 1, then E∘E, and log(1 + x) and log∘log below. It's one flow: E^0.7 after E^−0.3 equals E^0.4.

Rung s is just addition in the coordinate this surface gives at height s. So the sheet is what this flow does to straight lines; the next section checks that exactly.

Is the sheet the flow's "equals 1" slice? Not literally, but it is the same engine

The flow surface's own "equals 1" slice is a single curve: the x where Eˢ(x) = 1, which is x = E⁻ˢ(1), the path of 1 run backward (1.718 at s = −1, 1 at s = 0, ln 2 ≈ 0.693 at s = 1, 0.527 at s = 2).

The rung sheet is the "equals 1" level of the rung operation instead, and it is exactly the flow applied to straight lines: (a, b) is on the slice at height s exactly when E⁻ˢ(a) + E⁻ˢ(b) = E⁻ˢ(1). That holds at every height, whole or fractional, with no exceptions. I checked 26 heights to about 10⁻⁹, and the Lean file proves it for any lens (not compiled). The flow's curve tells you where the straight line sits, and the sheet's diagonal point is the flow carrying half of it back out. Turn the dial: the left panel bends, the right one stays straight.

What the flow adds: every other level (one nested sheet per value c), the actual value of any a ⊕ₛ b, and how points travel as the dial turns. The single sheet at c = 1 isn't enough on its own: I built a different, still associative rung ½ with exactly the same unit curve, which differs from the real one by up to 0.076 elsewhere and by 0.053 on its level-½ curve. All levels together are enough: halving with the rung operation alone gives the flow back to four or five decimals.

So they're one object. I've merged them in the ranking at 80%: the flow is the engine, and the sheet is its clearest picture.

The growing cube is a second dial

The cube whose dimension is a dial is real, but it turns a different knob. Combining 1 with itself d times by the circle operation gives 2ᵈ − 1, the corners of a d-cube, for any real d. That's a count, not a rung: the cube's dimension doesn't move you up the ladder.

Both dials live on one surface: Eˢ(d·E⁻ˢ(a)). Its rung-1 slice is the cube 2ᵈ − 1, its rung-0 slice is the line d, and at whole d it's d copies combined by rung s. Rung ½ run n times grows faster than every power of n and slower than every exponential (right chart, log scale).

The saddle and the helix

Saddle (20%)

The circle operation's graph z = a + b + ab is exactly a saddle, ruled by straight lines both ways. At the identity, rung s's graph is flat along the axes with twist s, so its curvature there is −s²/9. Only rungs 0 and 1 have straight rulings. Curvature can't tell s from −s; the mirror image does, which echoes your negatives-as-mirror idea. A reading of the sheet, not the sheet.

Helix (10%)

A complex number near 0 rides the rungs around a near-perfect circle that kisses 0 (2% off, from the x²/2 term of eˣ − 1). The whole ladder, from −∞ to +∞, is exactly one turn, then it's back at 0. So it's a single loop, not a repeating helix, and it never passes through the real rungs as curves. Lovely, but not the shape.

What the sheet says about infinities in between

Three different things get called "size of infinity", and the sheet speaks to one of them strongly.

Kind of sizeContinuous family?What the sheet adds
Cardinality (how many elements)No, with the axiom of choice cardinals come in steps; whether one sits between countable and the continuum can't be decided. Known, new, and proofs

  • Known: the smooth in-between rungs are the regular iteration of eˣ − 1 (Szekeres 1958, Écalle); the in-between ladder of "commutative hyperoperations" goes back to Bennett (1915); the tropical floor is Maslov's limit.
  • Possibly new, small: drawing every rung as one sheet with an exact area gauge (½ at addition, 2 ln 2 − 1 at the circle operation), and the two-dial surface joining the cube count to the rung height. I found no lead as promising as the solitons.
  • Proofs: RungShape.lean has 17 core-Lean theorems (twist = corner area, double ruling, exact unit curves at rungs 0 and 1, the near-identity flow's heights adding, the cube count 2ᵈ, and the sheet as the flow's pull-back of a straight line for any lens). No Lean here, so it is not compiled; the algebraic ones are also checked as exact sympy identities (20 of 20 pass).
