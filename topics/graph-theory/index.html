Series multiplies, parallel fuses, and the colour count picks the fuse

Wire bonds together and any series–parallel network collapses two bonds at a time. For reliability the rule is exact and old: series multiplies, parallel is the failure union a ∘₋₁ b. Give each site Q colours (the Potts model) and series still multiplies, while parallel becomes a landscape law. Every Q lands on one straight line of the landscape, the line of laws that can mean "or": Q = 1 is reliability, Q = 2 is velocity addition.

t is a bond's transmissivity, v/(Q + v); at Q = 1 it is the chance the bond works. The network collapses in three moves: the top pair in series, then the two branches in parallel, then the last bond in series.

The landscape: corner weight k across, bend q up. Grey curve: the border q = k²/4, where the two walls merge. Copper line: k + q = −1, every law with certainty as a wall. The dot is the parallel law for your Q; it slides from the cusp at Q = 0 through reliability (Q = 1) and velocity addition (Q = 2).

Reliability is exactly the dial pair (×, ∘₋₁)

series: p₁·p₂    parallel: p₁ ∘₋₁ p₂ = 1 − (1 − p₁)(1 − p₂)

Two bonds in series both have to work; in parallel at least one. On the failure side the two swap: a series pair fails by ∘₋₁ and a parallel pair fails by ×. That is De Morgan, the k = −1 lift x ↦ 1 − x. In odds against, u = 1/p − 1, a series pair is the plain circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0): u = u₁ ∘₁ u₂. So any network built from series and parallel pairs is a formula in × and ∘₋₁, checked here on 40 random networks against brute force.

Not every network is built that way. The Wheatstone bridge has no series or parallel pair at all. Pivot on its middle bond instead: contracted, it is two parallel pairs in series; deleted, two series pairs in parallel. That gives 2p² + 2p³ − 5p⁴ + 2p⁵. Pivoting is deletion–contraction, the move behind the Tutte polynomial.

With Q colours, parallel rides the landscape's "or" line

In the Potts model a bond carries weight v = e^J − 1, which is exactly the flow E applied to the coupling J. Couplings in parallel add, so weights combine by the circle operation: 1 + v = (1 + v₁)(1 + v₂). Series gives v₁v₂/(Q + v₁ + v₂), which looks messy until you switch to t = v/(Q + v). Then series is t₁t₂ and parallel is

t = (t₁ + t₂ + (Q − 2)t₁t₂) / (1 + (Q − 1)t₁t₂) = F_{Q−2, 1−Q}(t₁, t₂)

a group law at the landscape point (Q − 2, 1 − Q). Its two walls are a perfect bond (t = 1) and a forbidden bond, v = −1, which is how proper colourings are counted. It is also the union rule for two events with Ali–Mikhail–Haq dependence θ = 1 − Q, the same line the statistics page found. At Q = 2, t = tanh of the Ising coupling, and parallel bonds add couplings like relativity adds rapidities.

The diamond lattice is the dial pair, iterated

Replace every bond by a diamond: two paths of two bonds, in parallel. Do it again and again. Each round is one step of t ↦ F_{Q−2, 1−Q}(t², t²), so a huge lattice is solved by iterating one map of a single number. The curves show the rounds for the Q you chose above. Weak bonds flow to 0 (apart), strong ones to 1 (connected), and the switch sharpens at the fixed point t*, the critical point.

At Q = 1 the step is 2p² − p⁴ and t* is the golden ratio (√5 − 1)/2. The slope there, 4 − 4t* ≈ 1.528, gives the length exponent ln 2/ln 1.528 ≈ 1.635. At Q = 2, t* solves t³ + t² + t = 1 (0.5437); at Q = 3 it is exactly 1/2.

The partition function of this lattice was solved exactly this way (Berker and Ostlund 1979), and its complex zeros form a fractal Julia set (Derrida, De Seze and Itzykson 1983). That is the bridge to the Lee–Yang page.

The strong product of graphs is the Kronecker circle operation

In the strong product G ⊠ H two pairs are joined when each coordinate is equal or adjacent. Add the identity to each adjacency matrix (count "equal" as a step) and the product is a plain Kronecker product: (A + I) ⊗ (B + I). Subtract the I ⊗ I and the adjacency is A ⊗ I + I ⊗ B + A ⊗ B, the circle operation with matrices.

The three pieces are named products: A ⊗ I + I ⊗ B is the Cartesian product (the k = 0 part, no corner) and A ⊗ B the tensor product (the corner). Degrees fuse, d = d₁ ∘₁ d₂; eigenvalues fuse, λ ∘₁ μ; and the count 2m + n multiplies. The OR product (joined if either coordinate is) is the k = −1 version, with the all-ones matrix J in place of I.

The same split shows up in independence polynomials I(G), which count independent vertex sets by size. For a disjoint union I − 1 combines by the circle operation; for a join it just adds, since a set may use only one side. The Lee–Yang dial gas at k = 0 is exactly I(G).

P₃ ⊠ P₃: 12 Cartesian bonds (blue, the k = 0 part) and 8 diagonals (copper, the corner A ⊗ B). 20 = 3·2 + 3·2 + 2·2·2, and 2·20 + 9 = 49 = 7·7.

Tested and dropped as a lead: the Ihara zeta. Its formula 1/ζ(u) = (1 − u²)^(m−n) det(I − uA + u²(D − I)) checks out (also for a non-regular graph), but the only the circle operation in it is the Euler product over prime cycles, the same cube as the primes page. The landscape adds nothing there.

Lead worth following?

Yes: the diamond lattice on the landscape. It is the one place where the project's two operations, × and a landscape law, act together and produce something hard: an exact critical point and a fractal set of zeros, from iterating a single map. Everything is computable with tools already built: for each Q the map t ↦ F_{Q−2, 1−Q}(t², t²), its fixed point, and its Julia set.

The concrete test: draw the zero sets as Q slides along the "or" line, and see whether they pinch or change shape at the Beraha values Q = 4cos²(π/n) flagged on the Lee–Yang page. If they do, the landscape line organises colour counting across lattices; if not, the placement stays a tidy picture of known rules.
