In the complex plane, the circle operation is a spin and a stretch about the wall

Read a ✳ b = (1 + a)(1 + b) − 1 with complex numbers and it becomes a picture. Combining with w by the circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0) turns the whole plane about the point −1 by the angle of 1 + w and stretches it by the length of 1 + w. The wall is the one point that never moves, and almost everything complex analysis says about the circle operation is about hitting that point or winding around it.

Magenta dot: the wall −1. Circles and rays: distances and angles seen from the wall. Amber: w. Teal: a small triangle near z and its image after ✳ w. The two shaded triangles, (−1, 0, w) and (−1, z, z ✳ w), always have the same shape.

The step z ↦ z ✳ w, as seen from the wall

Seen from the wall, the circle operation is plain complex multiplication: z ✳ w + 1 = (1 + w)(z + 1). It is the old way of drawing a product with similar triangles, with the origin moved to −1 and the unit moved to 0.

The log of the lift turns the spin into a slide, and the wall is the branch point

Take L(z) = log(1 + z). Then L(z ✳ w) = L(z) + L(w), up to a whole number of turns 2πi. The way back is E(w) = e^w − 1, one step of the rung flow: it sends straight lines to the polar web about the wall. Vertical lines become circles round −1, horizontal lines become rays out of −1, and a climb of 2π up the strip is one full trip round the wall, landing back where it started (E(2πi) = 0).

So log(1 + z) has its branch point at the wall, not at 0. A loop round 0 that misses −1 changes nothing; any loop round −1 adds 2πi. The colour picture shows it: hue is the angle seen from the wall, and every hue meets at −1.

On the circle |1 + z| = 1, which passes through 0 with its centre on the wall, the circle operation just adds angles. That circle is the k = 1 rotation condition 2x + x² + y² = 0 from the linear algebra page.

Left: the w strip, a grid of plain addition. Right: its image under e^w − 1 over the phase colouring of 1 + z. Each coloured line on the left lands on the matching line on the right.

The circle roots of 0 sit on the circle through 0 around the wall

Combine z with itself n times by the circle operation and you get (1 + z)ⁿ − 1. That is 0 exactly when 1 + z is an n-th root of unity, so the n circle roots of 0 are ζ − 1: a regular n-gon on the circle |1 + z| = 1, with one corner at 0, the identity.

Combine all n of them by the circle operation and the answer is the product of the roots of unity, minus 1: 0 for odd n and −2 for even n, the point straight across the circle from 0. For n = 4 the square is 0, −1 + i, −2, −1 − i; for n = 3 the triangle is 0, ω − 1, ω² − 1. Both are proved in Lean on the Gaussian and Eisenstein integers.

Every landscape law is a Möbius flow whose fixed points are the walls

Fix a and look at b ↦ F(a, b) = (a + b + p·ab)/(1 − q·ab) as a map of the complex plane. It is a Möbius map with matrix [[1 + pa, a], [−qa, 1]], these matrices compose by the law itself, and their determinant 1 + pa + qa² is the wall polynomial. The fixed points of every one of these maps, whatever a is, are the two walls. The type is set by one number: trace² − 4·det = a²(p² − 4q).

The circle operation, walls −1 and ∞, complex a: orbits spiral out of the wall (loxodromic).

Velocity addition, walls ±1: orbits run on arcs from one wall to the other (hyperbolic).

Tangent addition, walls ±i: orbits circle the walls forever (elliptic).

Over the complex numbers this collapses the landscape to two laws. With two separate walls w₁, w₂, the coordinate ζ = (1 − x/w₁)/(1 − x/w₂) turns the law into multiplication, so the law is the circle operation in disguise (for tangent addition ζ is the Cayley map e^(2i·arctan x); for velocity addition it is the Doppler factor squared). When the two walls merge (p² = 4q) the law is plain addition in the coordinate x/(1 + cx). This is the textbook fact that a one-parameter Möbius group is conjugate to either z ↦ λz or z ↦ z + t.

The rung step e^z − 1 has a parabolic flower, and its two rulers disagree by a ripple

E(z) = e^z − 1 fixes 0 with slope exactly 1. In the complex plane that makes a Leau–Fatou flower with one attracting petal on the left (forward orbits creep into 0 like −2/n) and one repelling petal on the right (backward orbits, log(1 + z), creep in like 2/n).

Each petal has its own Abel ruler α, with α(E(z)) = α(z) + 1, and both start α(z) = −2/z + (1/3)·log z − z/36 + z²/540 + …. Where the petals overlap, above and below 0, the two rulers differ by a ripple a₁·e^(2πiw) with a₁ ≈ 10.7488 + 63.2463i (|a₁| ≈ 64.15), dying like e^(−2π·Im w) as you move toward 0.

Because the ripple is not zero, no single analytic fractional rung E^t lives on a whole neighbourhood of 0: every fractional rung is built petal by petal and the two sides glue smoothly but not analytically. This is the complex reason behind the divergence of the half-rung series that the fractional rungs section checked (Baker 1958; Écalle and Voronin around 1981).

A product's zeros are exactly where one piece hits the wall

An infinite product is a circle-operation fold: ∏(1 + aₙ) − 1 = a₁ ✳ a₂ ✳ a₃ ✳ …. Euler's sine product is the fold of the pieces −z²/n²:

sin(πz)/(πz) − 1 = (−z²) ✳ (−z²/4) ✳ (−z²/9) ✳ …

Its zeros are the points where one piece lands on the wall, −z²/n² = −1, that is z = ±n. Weierstrass's product for 1/Γ is the same with pieces (1 + z/n)e^(−z/n) − 1, which hit the wall at z = −n, the poles of Γ.

The fold converges when Σ|aₙ| does, because |a₁ ✳ … ✳ aₙ| ≤ |a₁| ✳ … ✳ |aₙ| (proved in Lean). A merely convergent Σaₙ is not enough: aₙ = (−1)ⁿ/√n sums fine, yet its product sinks to 0, because the corner terms aₙ²/2 pile up.

The argument principle counts wall hits too. The output-lifted derivative f′/(1 + f) of the calculus page, integrated round a loop and divided by 2πi, is the number of times f hits −1 inside. Round |z| = 3.5 the sine fold gives 6; e^z − 1 gives 0, because it never reaches the wall.

Lee–Yang zeros are wall hits on the dial

The Lee–Yang page already followed this to physics: the zeros of a dial gas's partition function, where 1 + (fold) vanishes, fill arcs that start at the wall. Nothing is repeated here; the counting tool above (winding round the wall) is the same one.

Glue the punctured plane and you get the elliptic page's torus

Remove the wall and the circle operation on what is left is the group of nonzero complex numbers. The log of the lift unrolls it to a cylinder of period 2πi; gluing 1 + a to q(1 + a) folds the cylinder into a torus, the Tate curve of the elliptic page. The gluing respects the circle operation (proved), so the torus inherits the law.

Lead worth following?

No new mathematical lead here. Complex analysis meets the circle operation as the group of nonzero complex numbers seen through the lift, and every result on this page is classical. What the branch gives the project is a sure geometric footing: the wall is a genuine puncture, the log of the lift is the cylinder over it, and the zeros, poles, roots and fixed points that showed up on other pages are all the same event, a piece landing on −1.

One thread has real content: the ripple between the two Abel rulers of the rung step. Its size says how far fractional rungs are from being one analytic flow, and it makes the choice of petal unavoidable. Follow it only if you want a canonical half rung; the answer is that none exists, and the ripple measures by how much.
