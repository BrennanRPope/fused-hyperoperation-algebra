Weight, bend and rung change the ruler. The nome is the one direction that changes the shape.

For a one-input law like the circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0) there are exactly three global shapes, told apart by how many periods the law's log has: none (addition), one (the circle operation), two (a torus). The only continuous dial left after that is the torus's proportions, the nome q. Weight is the size of the measuring stick, the landscape bend is a switch between line and circle, and rung height is a route through rulers that matches the bend to third order and leaves it at the fourth. New directions do appear, but only when there are two or more waves (pair weights) or when you insist on whole numbers (the weight's primes).

Each thin parabola is one shape seen through different rulers: the ruler x ↦ x/(1+cx) slides a law along its parabola and keeps its discriminant D = p² − 4d. Zooming the units multiplies D by λ², so after units only the sign of D is left: blue below the red border (two real walls, a line), amber above it (complex walls, a circle). The rung route lands on the parabola D = s, so to third order rung height is the bend's D. Below, its fourth-order coefficient −s(s−1)/12 is a height no point of the plane has; it crosses the plane only at rungs 0 and 1.

The whole shape is the number of periods

Every one-input law is plain addition seen through its log. What cannot be removed by any ruler is the set of periods of that log: the steps that bring you back to the start. That set is a lattice of rank 0, 1 or 2 (a classical theorem: the connected one-dimensional complex groups are ℂ, ℂ* and the tori). Counting the points that return to the start in n equal steps tells them apart: 1, n, n². Checked with division polynomials for n = 3 and 5 on the cusp, the node and the Tate torus at q = 0.1.

No period: addition

k = 0, the cusp y² = x³. Three steps back to the start: 1 point.

One period: the circle operation

Period 2πi/k. Every k ≠ 0 is the same shape after a zoom, so the weight is a size, not a direction. Three steps back: 3 points.

Two periods: a torus

The torus's shape is the ratio τ of its two periods; the nome is q = e^{2πiτ}. Stretching the torus (q → 0) pinches it into the circle operation's cylinder, which is the soliton edge of the theta lattice. The gluing a ↦ a ∘₁ (q − 1) commutes with the law for every q, so nothing near 0 can see the nome; only the whole torus can, through j.

Where new directions do appear

Several waves

A pair weight survives every separate rescaling of the waves (proved), so with g waves the period matrix has g(g+1)/2 entries: g nomes and g(g−1)/2 pair weights. The KdV collision weight A is the off-diagonal one at the soliton edge. From four waves on, Riemann's matrices outnumber the curves that can produce them (3g − 3): that gap is Schottky's problem, which Shiota settled in 1986 using exactly these soliton equations.

Whole numbers

Over the integers the weight is no longer just a size. The p-fold power of a + b + k·ab is 0 mod p when p divides k (the law looks like addition at p) and x^p mod p otherwise (it looks like the circle operation). So k carries its set of primes. This is Sekiguchi, Oort and Suwa's bridge from multiplication to addition.

kp = 2p = 3p = 5p = 7

On a graph

The same holds when one weight sits on many bonds at once: on three sites in a row, Z = 1 + 3z + (2k+1)z² + k²z³, and no rescaling of the sites changes k (proved). That is why the Potts colour count is physical even though a single law's weight is not.

Is any lead worth following?

  • Several waves: yes. This is the only place a whole new dimension actually appears, and the soliton pair weights already live there. The next test is concrete: three waves with three independent pair weights, and the count from the table above. It is known mathematics (Riemann, Krichever, Shiota), so it builds the map rather than discovering something, but it is where the map continues.
  • Rung height: not as a new direction. The third-order match D = s and the exact fourth-order gap −s(s−1)/12 are clean, but every rung is addition in its own ruler, so this route can never produce a new shape. It is worth following only if the question is about the ladder itself.
  • Whole numbers: a modest yes if you want the 2ⁿ − 1 counts and the integer side of the circle operation in the same picture. Known (Sekiguchi–Oort–Suwa), and it adds one discrete layer, not a dial.
  • Another dial for one-input laws: no. The classification is complete. A fourth shape would need a return count other than 1, n or n², which a one-dimensional group cannot have.

What would confirm a further direction

  • For one input: a smooth, associative, commutative law whose n-step return count is not 1, n or n². None exists, so the map for one input is closed.
  • For several waves: three or more waves whose pair weights cannot be set by one weight (already shown in the soliton work: KdV needs the triple corner A₁₂A₁₃A₂₃, a single law gives k²).
  • For non-commuting laws in three or more dimensions without rotational symmetry: Bianchi's classification has families with a free parameter, so a law that needs a direction-dependent twist would bring a genuine new dial. In the plane with rotational symmetry the twist adds nothing: it is curvature × area, one number.
