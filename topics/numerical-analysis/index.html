The lift costs digits; the fused form keeps them

The circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0) is (1 + a)(1 + b) − 1 = a + b + ab. On paper the two sides are equal. On a computer they are not: adding 1 moves a small number onto the coarse part of the float grid, where its last digits are rounded away, and subtracting 1 afterwards cannot bring them back. Written as a + b + ab, the corner is a small correction and every digit survives. This page checks that on real float64 and follows it into compound interest, rounding errors, Newton's method and Horner's rule.

A toy float system with 3 significant bits, so the ticks are visible. Ticks are the numbers it can hold: crowded near 0, a quarter apart between 1 and 2. The amber arrow is the lift 1 + a snapping to the nearest tick; the hollow dot is what comes back after subtracting 1. Real float64 has 53 bits, the same picture with ticks 2⁻⁵² apart near 1.

Why: floats are dense near 0 and sparse near 1

A float stores a fixed number of significant digits, so its grid spacing is proportional to the number's size. Near 10⁻¹² the spacing is about 10⁻²⁸; near 1 it is 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶. The lift x ↦ 1 + x is a translation: it carries 0, the identity, to 1, and with it carries every small number onto the coarse grid. The way back, subtracting 1, is exact, but by then the digits are gone.

The rule behind it is an amplifier. If the lift rounds by a relative amount η, then a comes back with relative error η·(1 + a)/a. Lean proves that identity (lift_amplifier); in float64 it holds to the last bit. At a = 10⁻¹² the amplifier is a trillion.

One fused step is one fma

Hardware fused multiply-add computes x·y + z with a single rounding (IBM POWER, 1990; standard in IEEE 754-2008). The circle operation has three fma shapes, all equal on paper and proved equal in Lean:

a ∘₁ b = fma(a, b, a + b) = fma(a, 1 + b, b) = fma(b, 1 + a, a)

For small a and b, a + b + ab and fma(a, b, a + b) do equally well: within one rounding for 400 random pairs at each size from 10⁻⁴ down to 10⁻¹⁶. The shapes with 1 + b inside round a lift first and lose a little (up to about 10⁻¹³ in the checks). The real difference shows up next, near the way back.

b is the inverse of a, −a/(1 + a), nudged by one part in 10⁹, so a ∘₁ b is tiny and everything cancels. a + b + ab   fma(a, b, a + b). Shaded: the octave 1/2 ≤ 1 + a ≤ 2.

Near the inverse, one fma is exact inside the octave around 1

When b is close to the inverse of a, a ∘₁ b is close to 0 and the two big pieces a + b and ab cancel. Plain a + b + ab rounds ab first and loses up to ten digits. fma(a, b, a + b) rounds ab not at all, so it is correct to the last bit whenever a + b itself is exact.

Sterbenz's lemma (1974) says x − y is exact when y/2 ≤ x ≤ 2y. Lean proves the inverse lands in that window whenever the lift 1 + a lies between 1/2 and 2 (sterbenz_window_pos, _neg). The checks agree: inside the shaded octave fma was correctly rounded 248 times out of 248; outside it, 572 of 952 were not. A geometric reading: stay within one factor of 2 of the identity in the lifted picture and one fma is perfect.

log1p and expm1 are the rung chart that keeps 0 at 0

The other way to turn ∘₁ into something easy is the log: log(1 + a ∘₁ b) = log(1 + a) + log(1 + b). Library functions log1p(x) = log(1 + x) and expm1(x) = eˣ − 1 compute these without ever forming 1 + x. expm1 is the rung flow E at t = 1 and log1p is its inverse, and both fix 0 with slope 1. So this chart turns the circle operation into adding without moving the identity onto the coarse grid. That is the whole reason they exist.

a^(∘₁t) = expm1(t · log1p(a))    x₁ ∘ₖ … ∘ₖ xₙ = expm1(Σ log1p(k·xᵢ)) / k

The second line is one routine for every dial value k. At k = −1 it is "at least one of many events": for 100,000 events of chance 10⁻¹², 1 − ∏(1 − p) is off by about 2 parts in 10⁷, while −expm1(Σ log1p(−p)) is right to 16 digits. A long product of 200,000 returns of size 10⁻⁷ tells the same story: naive product minus 1 keeps 9 digits, the fma fold 14, the log1p sum all 16.

Relative error of 5% a year compounded n times. (1 + r/n)ⁿ − 1   expm1(n·log1p(r/n)). Compounding every second, the naive formula keeps 7 digits.

Every rounding error is a circle-operation number

A float operation returns the true answer times (1 + δ) with |δ| ≤ u = 2⁻⁵³. Relative errors are lift coordinates, so they compose by the operation itself:

x(1 + δ₁) · y(1 + δ₂) = xy(1 + δ₁ ∘₁ δ₂)
x(1 + δ₁) / y(1 + δ₂) = (x/y)(1 + δ₁ ⊟ δ₂)

Division brings in circle-operation subtraction, Fisher's rule for real interest rates. A product of n floats carries Higham's total error θₙ, and θₙ is exactly the circle-operation fold δ₁ ∘₁ δ₂ ∘₁ … of the step errors: the checks compute each δ as an exact fraction and the fold matches θₙ to the last bit for n up to 1,000. Lean then proves Higham's bound in this form: if each |δ| ≤ u, the fold stays within u^(∘₁n) = (1 + u)ⁿ − 1, and that is at most nu/(1 − nu).

The bound is the worst case, every rounding in the same direction. Real roundings mostly cancel, like a random walk.

Where conditioning bites

Two points are dangerous, and they are the two special points of the circle operation. At the identity 0 the way back from the lift amplifies by (1 + a)/a; fused arithmetic avoids that. At the wall −1 the problem itself is touchy: the inverse −u/(1 + u) has relative condition 1/|1 + u| and log1p blows up too. No trick fixes the wall, because the answer genuinely depends wildly on the input there.

The unit square against the lifted rectangle (1 + e) × (1 − e). Slide the teal strip to the top and it fills all but the amber e × e corner, so the new residual is −e².

Newton's inverse without division: only the corner is left

To find the inverse v of u (so u ∘₁ v = 0, i.e. 1/(1 + u)) without dividing, repeat

v ← v ∘₁ (−e), where e = u ∘₁ v is the residual

Lean proves the new residual is exactly −e² (newton_residual), so after n steps it is −(−e₀)^(2ⁿ) (newton_iter_residual). In the lift the step multiplies by (1 − e), and (1 + e)(1 − e) = 1 − e²: the picture shows the e × e corner is the only thing that survives. That is quadratic convergence, digits doubling (0.9, 1.4, 2.3, 4.0, 7.6, 14.7, 16 in float64). It converges exactly when |e₀| < 1, i.e. 0 < (1 + u)(1 + v₀) < 2.

Goldschmidt's division (1964) is the same corner, unrolled: (−e) ∘₁ e ∘₁ e² ∘₁ e⁴ ∘₁ … = −e^(2ⁿ) (gold_eq).

Horner's rule walks the cube

Combining with b by the circle operation is the affine step acc ↦ acc·(1 + b) + b, which is one Horner step and one fma. So x₁ ∘₁ x₂ ∘₁ … ∘₁ xₙ in n fma steps adds up all 2ⁿ − 1 corners of the circle-operation cube (horner_cube), and the power a^(∘₁n) is Horner on b(1 + y + … + yⁿ⁻¹), y = 1 + b (mpow_horner).

Lead worth following?

A small practical lead, not a research one. The theory here is classical: numerical analysts have long known that adding 1 throws digits away and built log1p, expm1 and fma for it. What the circle operation adds is a single picture (the lift is a translation onto the coarse grid; the log chart is tangent to the identity) and one tidy rule (stay in the octave around 1 and one fma is exact).

That is worth turning into a tiny, tested kit, because it touches everything else in the project: multadd(a, b) as fma(a, b, a + b), and dialfold(k, xs) as expm1(Σ log1p(k·x))/k, with the wall −1/k handled explicitly. Any code that compounds rates, combines small probabilities or folds the cube would get every digit for free. It predicts nothing new about numbers, so I would not chase it as mathematics.
