Toric geometry and the primes

Your hunch is half right. Toric geometry, the shapes built from the circle-operation torus, makes every prime look exactly the same, which is why mathematicians call it geometry over the "field with one element", below all the primes. The primes show up one level down, in the windings x → xᵖ of the circle-operation circle: Riemann's zeta function counts those windings, the primes are the windings that can't be done in stages, and the field-with-one-element program is built on exactly them. Everything here is known math, and nothing here proves anything new.

Two leads worth your time, both already known

I looked for something as strong as the soliton match. Nothing here is new mathematics, but two matches tie your own pieces to the centre of this field as tightly as the soliton cube did.

Your tesseract coordinates are toric charts

The 16 corners you name by switches, with grade = dimension, are exactly the torus orbits of 4-space: a corner with k switches on is the set of points where those k coordinates are nonzero, and it holds (q − 1)ᵏ points over a field of size q. This is the orbit–cone correspondence (Section 1). Gluing such charts along a fan gives every toric variety, so the tesseract task and this one are the same object seen twice. That is the most solid road from your coordinates to "derive geometry from the circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0)". Section 7 glues four of these charts into P¹ × P¹ and Section 8 glues three into the projective plane, both checked in Lean.

The circle operation's own power is the field-with-one-element structure

Your Pascal cube's middle vanishes mod p: (1 + a)ᵖ − 1 ≡ aᵖ (mod p). Borger (2009) defines geometry over the field with one element as a family of windings, one per prime, each congruent to the p-th power mod p. The circle operation's p-th powers are that family for the torus. And it pins down primes exactly: the middle vanishes mod n only when n is prime, which is the identity behind the AKS primality test (2002). Section 3.

Over a finite field, points are counted in circle-operation coordinates

Work over a finite field with q elements (q is a prime p or a power of one). The circle-operation circle, every nonzero number, has q − 1 points. Call that number a. So a = q − 1 is the circle-operation coordinate and q = 1 + a is the ordinary one.

Your cube is 3-space, sorted by which coordinates are zero

Group the points of 3-space by which coordinates are nonzero. There are 8 groups, one per corner of your cube, and a corner with k switches on holds aᵏ points. Adding the corners gives 1 + 3a + 3a² + a³ = (1 + a)³ = q³, which is every point. The far corner, a³, is the torus itself.

Projective space is a circle power divided by a

Remove the origin and you have your circle power: a ∘₁ a ∘₁ a = (1 + a)³ − 1. Divide by the circle's scaling and you get the projective plane:

((1 + a)³ − 1) / a = a² + 3a + 3 = q² + q + 1

In the triangle that pictures it, the inside is the torus (a² points), each edge is a circle (a points), and each corner is one point.

A toric variety is these pieces glued along a fan, and its count is one polynomial

Each cone of the fan contributes one torus orbit with a(dimension left over) points, so every toric count is a polynomial in a. I counted points by brute force, every point of projective 3-space checked against the equation, for nine field sizes:

Toric shapes treat every prime alike, so their Riemann Hypothesis is automatic

Weil's zeta function packages the counts over q, q², q³, … into one function. For a toric shape, rewrite the count in the ordinary coordinate q: the coefficients become its Betti numbers h₀, h₁, … (for P¹ × P¹, (1 + q)² gives 1, 2, 1). Then:

Z(T) = 1 / [(1 − T)(1 − qT)²(1 − q²T)]

Checked against brute force over the fields of size 2, 4 and 8: 9, 25 and 81 points, as the formula says. Weil's Riemann Hypothesis asks that the numbers in those factors have size qi/2 in the i-th piece. Here they are exactly 1, q, q²: pure powers, no wobble at all. That is what "satisfies the Riemann Hypothesis trivially" means.

The rewrite from a to q is a known combinatorial move (the f-vector to h-vector transform). In your words: the count in circle-operation coordinates lists the cones, and the same count in ordinary coordinates lists the holes.

The field with one element is the limit a → 0

Because toric counts are polynomials, you can set q = 1, that is, a = 0. Every corner with a switch on vanishes and only the fixed points survive: the corners of the triangle, the vertices of the polytope. Tits noticed in 1957 that these limits look like counting over a field with one element.

The dream

Weil proved the Riemann Hypothesis for curves over finite fields by drawing the surface C × C, the diagonal, and the graph of Frobenius, and using a positivity of intersections. The hope is to see Spec ℤ, the set of all primes, as a curve over the field with one element, form "Spec ℤ × Spec ℤ" over it, and copy Weil's proof. The curve would have infinitely many holes, and its holes would be the zeta zeros: Weil's count qᵐ + 1 − Σ αᵐ has the same shape as Riemann's x − Σ xρ/ρ, the spiral sum on your prime page.

Where the circle operation sits

The torus is the circle-operation group, and toric varieties are the canonical shapes over F₁ in every version above.

Borger's windings are circle powers. His F₁ structure is a map ψₚ for each prime with ψₚ(a) ≡ aᵖ (mod p), all commuting. On the torus, ψₚ is x → xᵖ, which in your coordinate is a → (1 + a)ᵖ − 1. The congruence is your cube's middle vanishing mod p. For n = 4, 6, 8 and 9 the middle entries mod n include 2, then 3 and 2, then 4 and 6, then 3, so they are never all zero; for every prime up to 199 they are all zero. That fact, n is prime exactly when (1 + a)ⁿ ≡ 1 + aⁿ (mod n), is what Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena turned into the first fast, certain primality test.

Primes are the windings of the circle that can't be done in stages

The maps from the circle-operation circle to itself are the windings x → xⁿ. Doing n and then m is the winding by nm. A winding by n can be done in stages through any divisor of n, and the stages form a box: for 30, the cube of 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30, with each edge a prime winding. A prime is a winding with no stage in between. Unique factorization says every winding breaks into prime stages in one way, up to order.

Zeta counts the windings

The circle has one winding of each degree, so Σ n−s = ζ(s) counts them, and the Euler product says you pick each prime's winding independently. That is the cube from your prime page, ζ(s) − 1 = the circle operation of all the 1/(pˢ − 1), one per prime p, now with a picture behind it.

Counting over a prime is counting fixed points of a winding

Over a field of size q, the points are the fixed points of Frobenius, x → xq. Over the complex numbers, the same winding z → zq fixes 0, ∞ and the q − 1 roots of unity: q + 1 points, the count of P¹. On every toric shape the q-fold winding's fixed points match the count over the field of size q, orbit by orbit, (q − 1)ᵏ each. This is the Lefschetz fixed-point picture, and it is standard.

On the n-torus, windings count like toric points

The ways to wind the n-torus onto itself (sublattices of ℤⁿ) are counted by ζ(s)ζ(s − 1)…ζ(s − n + 1), a classical formula with five proofs in Lubotzky and Segal's book. That is exactly the arithmetic zeta function of the toric shape Pⁿ⁻¹. So the prime windings of the 2-torus number p + 1, the points of P¹ over p. Checked by listing every sublattice up to index 30:

The primon gas

Treat each winding as a state of a gas whose particles are the prime windings, each carrying energy log p. A winding by n is a state with the particles of n's factorization. The partition function at temperature 1/β is Σ n−β = ζ(β), and the pole at β = 1 is a phase transition (Julia 1990). Bost and Connes (1995) added the roots of unity, the circle-operation circle's torsion, and got a system whose symmetry group is the Galois group of all roots of unity. Connes, Consani and Marcolli read it as an F₁ object. It is the most developed version of "primes as irreducible windings", and it is known.

Each prime winds the roots of unity its own way

Frobenius at p sends each n-th root of unity ζ to ζᵖ. On the circle that is winding p times, the dial at the top. Three things fall out:

Fixed points. The dots Frobenius leaves alone are the roots of unity that already exist in the field of size p. There are gcd(n, p − 1) of them, so all n exist exactly when p ≡ 1 (mod n).

Splitting. Frobenius moves the primitive roots in loops of equal length, the order of p mod n. The prime p breaks into φ(n) ÷ (loop length) primes in the field of n-th roots of unity. This is Kummer's and Dedekind's theory.

Folding. When p divides n, the winding sends several dots to one. Those are the ramified primes, the only ones where the picture breaks.

Only p mod n matters, and every allowed remainder gets a fair share of primes (Dirichlet 1837, Chebotarev 1922). By Kronecker–Weber, every commutative way of splitting primes comes from such a dial.

Is this "how the primes connect"?

It is how they connect to each other through shared symmetry: p and p′ behave the same on the n-dial when p ≡ p′ (mod n). This is the commutative half of the story, and it is all the circle operation. What it does not give is where the primes sit on the number line. That lives in the zeta zeros.

Over the integers, a toric shape's zeta is Riemann's, copied

Multiply a toric shape's zeta functions over all primes at once and you get shifted copies of Riemann's zeta, one per Betti number:

ζP¹×P¹(s) = ζ(s) · ζ(s − 1)² · ζ(s − 2)

The torus itself gives ζ(s − 1) / ζ(s). Checked for P¹ × P¹ at p = 2 by summing the counts (2ᵐ + 1)² against the product: 1.6743930 both ways.

So the zeros sit on the lines Re s = ½, 3/2, 5/2, if and only if Riemann's own zeros sit on Re s = ½. Toric geometry doesn't escape the Riemann Hypothesis; it copies it once per hole dimension. The hard part lives in Spec ℤ, the primes themselves, not in any toric shape.

Your tesseract charts, glued into P¹ × P¹ and checked in Lean

P¹ × P¹ is four copies of the plane, one per quadrant of its fan. Each copy is a chart with your corner coordinates: switch i is on when coordinate i is nonzero. Charts are glued by x ↦ 1/x on whichever factor changes.

The gluing map is the circle inverse

Write x = 1 + u. Then 1/x = 1 + u′ with u′ = −u/(1 + u), the u′ that makes u ∘₁ u′ = 0. The one point the inverse can't reach, u = −1, is x = 0, and that is exactly the point the other chart sees as infinity.

(1 + u)⁻¹ = 1 + (−u / (1 + u))

Your 1/x wall is where you change charts. That is standard geometry, not a new theorem, but it puts the wall in a place where nothing breaks.

What the Lean file proves

For any field (the rationals, or any field in mathlib):

1. A point's corner never changes under torus scaling, so corners label the orbits.
2. Each of the four charts is one-to-one, and together they cover every point.
3. Where two charts overlap, the transition carries one chart's coordinates to the other's.
4. Going chart c → d → e equals c → e (the cocycle condition), so the gluing is consistent.
5. Each chart's origin is a fixed point lying in that chart only; torus points lie in all four.
6. The fan count a² + 4a + 4 = (a + 2)², the circle-operation form of (q + 1)².

The projective plane: three charts whose gluing mixes the coordinates

The fan of the projective plane P² has three rays, e₁, e₂ and −e₁ − e₂, and three cones that are not quadrants. So P² is not a product, and changing charts no longer flips one coordinate at a time. The charts see (x/z, y/z), (z/x, y/x) and (z/y, x/y), and going from the first to the second is

(a, b) ↦ (1/a, b/a)

The new coordinate b/a uses both old ones. Every chart change here is a monomial map, a 2×2 matrix of whole numbers acting on the exponents.

In circle-operation coordinates the gluing is circle-operation division

Write a = 1 + u and b = 1 + w. The chart change becomes: take the circle inverse of u, then combine it with w by the circle operation.

(u, w) ↦ (u⁻, w ∘₁ u⁻),   u⁻ = −u / (1 + u)

On P¹ × P¹ the gluing was the inverse alone. On P² it is the inverse and then a circle operation, which is the circle operation's version of dividing. The 1/x wall u = −1 is again where you change charts.

Windings pass through every chart

Winding x ↦ xⁿ commutes with every chart change, because powers commute with monomial maps. So [x : y : z] ↦ [xⁿ : yⁿ : zⁿ] is one map of the whole plane, read the same way in every chart. Over a field with q elements its fixed points number g² + 3g + 3 with g = gcd(n − 1, q − 1). That is the P² count for a field of size g + 1. When n = q the winding is the Frobenius and fixes all q² + q + 1 points.

Winding n (q = 13)gFixed points= count at size g + 1

Reading the fixed points as "P² over 0 and the g-th roots of unity" is the known field-with-one-element picture (Kapranov and Smirnov; Connes and Consani), not a new result. The counts were computed by brute force.

What the Lean file proves

For any field:

1. Each of the three charts is one-to-one, and together they cover every point of P².
2. A point of one chart lies in another exactly where the transition can divide, and there the transition carries one chart's coordinates to the other's.
3. The cocycle condition: going chart c → d → e equals c → e on every triple overlap.
4. Each chart's origin lies in that chart only; torus points lie in all three.
5. In circle-operation coordinates the chart changes are (u, w) ↦ (u⁻, w ∘₁ u⁻) and (u, w) ↦ (w⁻, u ∘₁ w⁻).
6. Windings commute with every chart change and give one well-defined map of P².
7. The fan count a² + 3a + 3 = q² + q + 1.

Where this leaves the hunch, and what to do next

Toric geometry is the right language for the commutative, circle-operation half of the primes: the dials, the windings, the Euler cube, the F₁ limit. It is blind to where the primes sit; that information is the zeta zeros, which no toric shape has. Three next steps fit your rule of a geometric underpinning you can trust:

1. Done for P¹ × P¹ (Section 7) and the projective plane (Section 8); the general case is any fan, which is where mathlib would help. 2. Draw the Frobenius dial and the Euler cube as one picture of "the commutative primes". 3. Try graphs: on a finite graph, primes are the loops that aren't repeats of shorter loops, Ihara's zeta counts them, and its Riemann Hypothesis holds exactly for Ramanujan graphs (Ihara 1966, Sunada). It is the one place where "primes are irreducible windings" and the Riemann Hypothesis are both pictures you can compute.
