Sugeno's λ-measure is the dial: probability with a weighted corner

An ordinary measure adds: two parcels that don't overlap weigh a + b. Sugeno's λ-measure (1974) weighs them a + b + λab. That is mult-add's dial with k = λ, and nothing else. So its whole theory, from the normalizing equation to its dual, is the dial's algebra drawn on one rectangle. Below, measure comes first; independence and growth processes follow from it.

The lift: a rectangle with sides 1 + λa and 1 + λb. The dashed square is 1. The two strips are λa and λb, and the orange corner is λ²ab. When λ is negative the strips are cut away, so their overlap would be cut twice; the corner puts it back once.

μ(A ∪ B) for disjoint A, B

1 + λ·μ(A∪B) = (1 + λa)(1 + λb)

The area of the rectangle is the lift of the union. Lifts multiply, so the measure of a union is a fold of the dial over its atoms, and order never matters.

The masses are the corners of the mult-add cube

Give each atom (single point) a density gᵢ. Expanding the lift (1 + λg₁)(1 + λg₂)(1 + λg₃) visits every corner of the cube once, exactly as the mult-add cube does. Divide by λ and the measure of a set A is a sum over the corners S inside A:

μ(A) = ΣS ⊆ A λ|S|−1·∏i∈S gᵢ

Those corner amounts are what fuzzy-measure theory calls the Möbius masses: how much weight sits on each group of atoms jointly. Layer one is the atoms, layer two is pairs weighted λ, layer three is the triple weighted λ². When λ ≥ 0 every mass is positive, which makes μ a belief function in the Dempster–Shafer sense. When λ is negative the masses alternate in sign, and the dual measure 1 − μ(Aᶜ) is the belief function instead.

A small tie to the q-numbers: m equal atoms of size g have μ = g·[m]q with q = 1 + λg. Counting atoms under the dial is q-counting.

Densities 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, which forces λ ≈ 0.372. Each corner shows its mass; the circle's area is proportional to it. The seven non-empty corners together weigh exactly 1.

The weight is not free. Solid: ∏(1 + λgᵢ). Dashed: 1 + λ. They meet at λ = 0 and at one more point, the weight. Orange: densities 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 (sum below 1, so λ > 0). Teal: 0.4, 0.5, 0.6 (sum above 1, λ ≈ −0.772).

Normalizing fixes the weight: total area must be 1 + λ

For μ(Ω) = 1 the whole rectangle must have area 1 + λ, so the densities decide λ through Sugeno's equation:

(1 + λg₁)(1 + λg₂)⋯(1 + λgₙ) = 1 + λ

There is exactly one nonzero root above −1 (checked on 400 random spaces). If the densities add to less than 1 the parcels must gain, so λ > 0; if they add to more, λ < 0. For two atoms Lean solves it outright: λ = (1 − g₁ − g₂)/(g₁g₂).

The two signs have plain meanings. With λ negative, let atom i "fire" by itself with chance −λgᵢ. Then μ(A) is the chance some atom of A fires, as a share of the chance any fires: the failure union, rescaled. With λ positive, μ(A) is the compound growth of A's atoms as a share of the whole: interest compounding.

Take the log and it is ordinary probability; μ is a q-number of P

The rung-0 ruler for the dial is log(1 + λx). Rescale it so the whole space is 1:

P(A) = log(1 + λμ(A)) / log(1 + λ)

P adds over disjoint sets: it is an honest probability. Turning it around, μ(A) = (qP(A) − 1)/(q − 1) with q = 1 + λ = k + 1, which is the q-number [P]q at a real argument. So a λ-measure is a probability measure read through the q-number lens, and q = k + 1 is exactly the project's convention. Distorting a probability this way is known; the q-number name for it is just the project's reading.

Inclusion–exclusion becomes multiplication. For overlapping sets the lift obeys (1 + λμ(A∪B))(1 + λμ(A∩B)) = (1 + λμ(A))(1 + λμ(B)), and for more sets each corner's lift appears with power ±1. The signs of the classical formula moved into exponents.

The dual weight is mult-add's inverse. The dual measure μ*(A) = 1 − μ(Aᶜ) is again a λ-measure, with weight λ* = −λ/(1 + λ). That is mult-add's way back for λ, so q* = 1/q and the dual of the dual is the original. Lean proves this from the normalizing equation.

μ against P for q = 0.2, 0.5, 2, 5 (λ = q − 1). q = 1 is the diagonal, plain probability. Duals are mirror images through the centre: q and 1/q.

The landscape plane, dial p across, bend q up. Shaded: laws that can carry a normalized measure on finitely many atoms. Its edge is the "or" line k + q = −1 from the statistics page, until the cusp (−2, 1), then the border parabola, which the line touches there.

Why λ must exceed −1: the wall has to stay outside [0, 1]

Sugeno's definition requires λ > −1. The dial explains the rule. Its wall sits at −1/λ, and values below a wall stay below it, because positive lifts multiply to positive lifts. When λ ≤ −1 the wall sits inside (0, 1], so no collection of parcels can ever reach measure 1. At exactly λ = −1 the only way to 1 is a sure atom. Lean proves all three steps.

The same test works on every law of the landscape, F(a, b) = (a + b + p·ab)/(1 − q·ab). Measures that combine by such a law are Weber's decomposable measures (1984). A finite normalized one exists exactly when the additive ruler L(x) = ∫dt/(1 + pt + qt²) stays finite up to 1, which happens exactly when 1 + pt + qt² has no root in [0, 1]. On the map, the edge of that region is the statistics page's "or" line, then the border parabola, meeting tangentially at the cusp. So every law that can serve as "A or B" sits exactly on the edge: certainty is its wall, so it can be approached but not reached from finitely many uncertain parts.

Inside the region there are bent examples. The tangent law (0, 1) gives μ = tan(πP/4): disjoint sets combine as (μA + μB)/(1 − μA·μB) and the whole space has measure 1.

Independence: expectation passes straight through the dial

For independent X and Y and any weight k, E[X ✳ₖ Y] = E X ✳ₖ E Y. The reason is the corner: the only cross term is k·XY, and independence makes E[XY] = E X·E Y. For dependent variables the gap is exactly k·Cov(X, Y), the corner's excess. Lean proves both on finite distributions. The statistics page had this for events (the union law with k = −lift); this is the same fact for any random quantity.

Odds of an "or" mult-add. For independent events the lift of the odds is 1 + odds = 1/(1 − p), and misses multiply, so

odds(A or B) = odds A ✳ odds B

and odds against an "and" mult-add the same way. A 30% and a 45% event: odds 0.4286 ✳ 0.8182 = 1.597, matched by 400,000 simulated draws.

Growth: martingales and Yor's formula are the cube in time

Wealth after returns r₁, r₂, … is r₁ ✳ r₂ ✳ ⋯. With independent fair returns (mean 0) the expected change stays 0 at every step, by the engine above: a multiplicative martingale. Yet six fair ±50% bets leave the typical path down 58%. The mean is a dial of means; the typical path follows the log ruler, where each step loses ½·log(0.75).

In continuous time this is the stochastic exponential ℰ (Doléans-Dade 1970). Yor's formula ℰ(X)·ℰ(Y) = ℰ(X + Y + [X, Y]) is mult-add one step at a time: each step's increment is Δx ✳ Δy, and the bracket [X, Y] is the running total of corner cells ΔxΔy. For Brownian motion the self-corners ΣΔW² add up to time t, and half of them is the familiar drift correction: ℰ(σW) = exp(σW − σ²t/2).

One path, 4,000 steps. Orange: the product ∏(1 + σΔW). Teal dashed: exp(σW − σ²t/2), on top of it. Grey: exp(σW), which forgets the corners and drifts away.

Lead worth following?

A small one, yes. The dial is not just present in measure theory, it is a named object there: Sugeno's λ-measure is the dial, and every classical fact about it is a line of dial algebra. That gives the project a ready, honest home in measure theory, and the Lean file already formalizes λ-measures.

The step worth taking next is the bent laws. The landscape picks out a two-parameter family of decomposable measures with a clean test for which ones exist, and its edge is the same "or" line the statistics page found. The open question is concrete: what do integrals against a bent measure (the Choquet integral, which averages by layers) look like, and do they reproduce known aggregation rules? That is checkable in an afternoon.

It will not predict new probability. The facts are known; what is ours is the single rectangle-and-cube picture and the placement on the map.
