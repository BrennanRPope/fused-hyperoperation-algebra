Lead check: not a lead

The overlap-as-cosine and band-as-country readings are exact and tie this subject to the conservation page, but they are the standard transfer-matrix picture of tight binding renamed. The bond-ladder test is a genuine check of the dial and it fails outside carbon, so it gives nothing to follow.

Overlap is a cosine, and the bond follows the same law as a bound mass

Mix two atomic orbitals as a φa + b φb. Their overlap S works as the cosine between them, and the size of the mixture is

|a φa + b φb|² = a² + b² + 2ab·S

That is the same law of cosines as the invariant mass on the previous page, M² = m₁² + m₂² + 2m₁m₂c. S = 1 is the border, where the two orbitals are one and their weights simply add. |S| < 1 is the turning country. For hydrogen's 1s orbitals at the H₂ bond length, S = 0.753.

The cosine also explains a textbook asymmetry. Solving H c = E S c gives a bonding level (α + β)/(1 + S) and an antibonding level (α − β)/(1 − S). The antibonding one is pushed up more than the bonding one comes down, by exactly (1 + S)/(1 − S): about 7 times for H₂ in the extended Hückel estimate. That is why filling both (as in He₂) costs energy overall.

A carbon chain is the landscape point (x, 1): band, edge and gap are the three countries

In Hückel theory a chain of carbons obeys one recurrence: the polynomial whose roots are the levels of an N-carbon chain satisfies P(N + 2) = x·P(N + 1) − P(N), with x the energy in units of β. That is the landscape recurrence with k = x and q = 1. Lean proves its invariant P(N+1)² − P(N+2)P(N) equals qN+1 in general, so a plain chain keeps it at exactly 1 at every length.

The three countries are the three things an electron can do along the chain:

  • Turning, |x| < 2: the step multiplier is a rotation, so the wave travels. Every level of every chain lands here, at 2cos(jπ/(N+1)) (the picture at the top).
  • Border, |x| = 2: the band edges, where the root is double.
  • Two walls, |x| > 2: a real pair λ, 1/λ, so the wave dies off by arccosh(|x|/2) per carbon. These are gaps.

Alternate the bonds (long, short, long, as in polyacetylene) and the two-step transfer still has q = 1, but a gap of width 2|t₁ − t₂| opens in the middle of the band, inside the two-walls country. A 200-carbon chain shows it at 0.404 against the predicted 0.400.

How fast a state dies off per carbon, against its energy. Inside the band (blue) it doesn't die at all. Bond alternation (red) opens a two-walls gap in the middle, the semiconductor gap of polyacetylene.

Rings: 4n + 2 and 4n are the two ways to close the chain

Close the chain into a ring and the levels become 2cos(2πj/N), the vertices of a polygon in a circle (Frost's picture). A plain ring has one bottom level and then pairs, so it closes its bonding shell at 2 + 4m electrons: Hückel's rule. Give the ring one twist (a Möbius ring) and every level comes in a pair, so it closes at 4m. Checked for every ring of 3 to 19 atoms, and Lean proves the two counts.

Benzene's six π electrons give 8β, which is 2β more than three separate double bonds: its aromatic stabilization. Cyclobutadiene's four give exactly 4β, nothing extra, with two electrons left unpaired on the middle line. Butadiene, the open chain, gains 0.472β.

Frost diagrams: each ring's levels sit at the corners of a polygon in a circle, vertex down for a plain ring and edge down for a Möbius ring. Levels below the yellow zero line are bonding; arrows are electrons.

Bond order counts bonding minus antibonding pairs, and N₂ has three

Fill the second-row diatomics' valence orbitals and halve the difference between bonding and antibonding electrons: Li₂ 1, Be₂ 0, B₂ 1, C₂ 2, N₂ 3, O₂ 2, F₂ 1, Ne₂ 0. Those match the measured bonds, including O₂'s two unpaired electrons. This is plain counting, with no circle operation (called mult-add before 1.0) in it.

One dial does not describe single, double and triple bonds, and nitrogen shows why

Bond energies don't add: a double bond is not two single bonds. The natural the circle operation test is to treat each extra bond as a weighted circle operation with one dial k, fixed by the single and double bonds, and see if it predicts the triple. Using the LibreTexts table of average bond enthalpies:

  • C–C: predicts 817 kJ/mol against 839 (-3%).
  • C–N: predicts 969 against 891 (+9%).
  • N–N: predicts 817 against 941 (-13%).
  • C–O: predicts 1342 against 1072 (+25%).

So the dial fits carbon's ladder and fails the rest. Nitrogen is the instructive failure. Carbon's increments shrink (348, 266, 225 kJ/mol), but nitrogen's grow (163, 255, 523): its single bond is weak (lone pairs on neighbouring atoms repel) and its π bonds are strong. Breaking N≡N down to N=N costs 523 of the 941 kJ/mol, 56%. That first step is why nitrogen fixation is hard, for the Haber process and for nitrogenase alike. The thermodynamics and the rate of that step come back in subjects 7 and 9.
