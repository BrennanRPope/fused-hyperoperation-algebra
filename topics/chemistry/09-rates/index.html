Lead check: not a lead

Second-order kinetics composing by an exact border law is a clean reading I haven't seen stated, but it is the familiar fact that 1/[A] grows linearly, so it predicts nothing new. The osculating curve for order n is a curiosity that describes nothing away from zero conversion. Neither is worth following.

First order: consecutive times and parallel channels both compose at k = −1

For a first-order reaction the fraction left after time t is e−kt, so the fractions left over consecutive times multiply and the conversions combine by failure-union, X(t₁ + t₂) = X(t₁) ⊞₋₁ X(t₂). Two parallel channels add their rate constants (the k = 0 law), so again the surviving fractions multiply and the conversions combine at k = −1. The branching ratio between the channels stays fixed at every time.

Second order is an exact landscape law on the border

For 2A → products, 1/[A] grows linearly in time, so the conversion's odds X/(1 − X) add over consecutive times. Composing two conversions therefore gives

X(t₁ + t₂) = (X₁ + X₂ − 2X₁X₂)/(1 − X₁X₂)

which is the landscape law at (k, d) = (−2, 1). That point is on the border k² = 4d, the same country as the flat triangle and Lavoisier's mass law. Zeroth order composes by plain addition, (0, 0), and first order by (−1, 0). All three are checked against numerical integration, and Lean proves the second-order identity.

Order n is a Tsallis q-exponential, and only orders 0, 1 and 2 are landscape laws

For a reaction of order n the fraction left is the Tsallis q-exponential eq(−kt) with q = n, and consecutive times compose by Tsallis's q-product. Behind this sits a clean identity: the q-logarithm turns ordinary products into the dial, lnq(xy) = lnqx ⊞1−q lnqy.

Is every order a landscape law? No. Fitting the landscape form to third-order kinetics needs a q that drifts from 2.31 to 2.92 across conversions, so it is not one law. Near zero conversion, though, order n always touches the landscape at (−n, n(n − 1)/2), which gives exactly (0, 0), (−1, 0) and (−2, 1) for the first three orders. That curve's discriminant is n(2 − n): two walls for orders between 0 and 2, the border at 0 and 2, and the turning country above 2. This is a small curiosity, not a lead: away from zero conversion it describes nothing.

The wall (all reactant gone) is reached in finite time exactly when n < 1: order ½ empties at kt = 2, order 0 at kt = 1, while orders 1 and up only approach it (picture at the top).

The landscape plane. Orders 0, 1 and 2 are exact landscape laws (blue dots), with order 2 on the border. Other orders only touch the dashed curve (−n, n(n−1)/2) near zero conversion; order 3 (red) is shown where it touches.

Arrhenius: barriers add in the exponent, so rates multiply, and nitrogen fixation needs both heat and a catalyst

The rate constant k = A·e−Ea/RT puts barriers and inverse temperatures in the exponent, so rate ratios over successive changes multiply. At 700 K a catalyst that lowers a barrier by 10, 50 or 100 kJ/mol multiplies the rate by 5.6, 5.4e+03 and 2.9e+07.

This closes the nitrogen-fixation thread. Thermodynamics (subject 7) wants ammonia made below about 463 K, but the N≡N bond's hard first step (subject 4) is far too slow there. Raising the temperature to 700 K speeds a 100 kJ/mol step by about 7e+03, at the cost of equilibrium yield, which the high pressure (subject 8) wins back. The iron catalyst lowers the barrier so that this compromise works at all.
