Calculus is mult-add with the corner dropped

Grow a box by a small step and it gains two strips and one corner square. Mult-add keeps the corner. Calculus is what is left when the step shrinks to nothing and the corner goes with it.

What the box says

The box has sides F = 3 and G = 2. One step grows them by ΔF = 1.2h and ΔG = 0.9h.

Strip ΔF·G Strip F·ΔG Corner ΔF·ΔG Corner's share of the growth

Divide by h and slide h toward 0: the strips become Leibniz's rule (FG)′ = F′G + FG′, and the corner shrinks like h² and disappears. That one disappearance is the only place a limit enters.

Relative growth says the same thing. Rates p and q over a step of size h give the product the rate p + q + h·p·q, your weighted mult-add with the step size as the corner weight.

The dictionary, rule by rule

Write a step as f(k+1) = f(k) ⊞ d(k). Then d is the mult-add slope, and through the lift 1 + x every step multiplies a box by (1 + d).

The fundamental theorem is the cube's lift

Mult-add the slopes back together and you get the whole climb: f(n) = f(0) ⊞ (d₀ ⊞ d₁ ⊞ … ⊞ dₙ₋₁). Through the lift it says the end box is the start box times every step's factor. That is Volterra's product integral (1887), exact at every step size, and proved in Lean.

A constant slope a gives a ⊞ a ⊞ … ⊞ a, the mult-add power. So the straight lines of mult-add calculus are exponentials. Slice a total rate x into n steps of x/n and the staircase closes on eˣ − 1, which the drawing shows for x = 1.

The ordinary version is the same telescope with + in place of ⊞: the differences add back to f(n) − f(0). Also proved.

Black curve: eˣ − 1. Grey stairs: 2, 4 and 16 mult-add steps of size x/n.

Four ways to fuse a derivative

The lift x ↦ 1 + x can go on the output, the input, both or neither. Each choice is an existing calculus, shifted by 1.

Lifted sideStepDerivative it givesKnown as
Neither(f(x+h) − f(x)) / hf′Newton–Leibniz
Output(f(x+h) ⊟ f(x)) / hf′ / (1 + f)Geometric calculus (Grossman–Katz, 1972); its exponential form is Volterra's
Input(f(x ⊞ h) − f(x)) / (x ⊞ h − x)Jackson's q-derivative, q = 1 + hq-calculus (Jackson, 1908)
Both(f(x ⊞ h) ⊟ f(x)) / h(1 + x) f′ / (1 + f)Bigeometric calculus (Grossman–Katz)

The q-derivative does appear, as you asked to check: stepping the input by mult-add moves it by h(1 + x), which is Jackson's gap with q = 1 + h. In the output-lifted calculus the product rule is just the sum rule, D(f ⊞ g) = Df + Dg, and the chain rule is D(f∘g) = (Df)(g)·g′. In exponential form that chain rule raises to the power g′, so it needs rung 3.

Your nth-derivative formula is the cube's layers

Your original formula wasn't kept, but it matched the binomial theorem and Taylor's series, so this builds on the binomial form, Newton's forward formula: f(n) = Σ C(n,k)·Δᵏf(0). The n-th difference is a signed walk over the n-cube's layers, and the layers weigh the differences when you rebuild f. Lean proves this exactly.

Now shrink the step to x/N. The layer weight C(N,k)·(x/N)ᵏ closes on xᵏ/k!, the same collapse that builds e on the e page, and Δᵏf/hᵏ closes on the k-th derivative. Newton's formula turns into Taylor's series. That last move is a limit, so it is checked numerically, not in Lean.

The strongest lead: time-scale calculus

Time-scale calculus is weighted mult-add calculus, with the corner weight equal to the step size. Hilger (1988) and Bohner and Peterson (2001) built one calculus that covers both sums and integrals. Its ⊕ is p ⊕ q = p + q + μpq, where μ is the step size. Its inverse is ⊖p = −p/(1 + μp). It works only off the wall 1 + μp = 0, and its exponentials obey e_p·e_q = e_(p⊕q).

That is your weighted family a + b + k·ab, with k = μ, your wall at −1/k and your lift. Step size 0 gives plain + and ordinary calculus. Step size 1 gives plain mult-add and difference equations. The field is established, so this is solid ground for "calculus falls out", not new math. It is nowhere near as surprising as the soliton cube.

Known and new

Known: every rule on this page. Product integrals (Volterra 1887), geometric and bigeometric calculus (Grossman and Katz 1972), the q-derivative (Jackson 1908), Newton's forward formula and time scales (Hilger 1988). The corner-square picture of the discrete product rule is textbook too.

New here: one picture that holds all of them. The four calculi are the four places to put x ↦ 1 + x. Discrete and continuous calculus are two slices of one weighted mult-add family, where the step size is the corner weight. And the limit enters calculus at one place only: when the corner weight goes to 0.

Inferred, not proved: that nothing else in single-variable calculus needs more than rungs 2 and 3 plus that one limit. It held for every rule I tried.
