Give each side a "nothing" and mult-add becomes a plain product

A ✳ B means: something from A only, something from B only, or one of each. That is a + b + ab choices. Add a "nothing" value to each side (Option, Maybe) and pairs of options are exactly those choices plus one empty pair. This is the lift 1 + x, made out of sets, and in the category of partial functions A ✳ B is the honest categorical product.

The grid is (a + 1) by (b + 1): that is the lift, (1 + a)(1 + b). Strike out the grey corner and what is left is a ✳ b. The coloured edges are the wedge (a + b), the gold block is the smash (ab). Topologists use those two words for exactly these two pieces.

In code it is These, align, and the full outer join

Haskell calls A ✳ B These a b (the these package); Rust's itertools calls it EitherOrBoth. The Option fact is a two-way conversion that loses nothing, and Lean checks both round trips:

inductive These (α β : Type) where
  | this : α → These α β        -- A only
  | that : β → These α β        -- B only
  | both : α → β → These α β    -- one of each

def optionThese :
  Iso (Option (These α β)) (Option α × Option β)

Zipping two lists of different lengths without dropping the overhang (align, zip_longest) returns a list of These. Lean proves that both input lists come back out, the length is the longer one, and the ordinary zip is just the "both" part, the ab corner.

A full outer join by key gives each key a These too: in the left table only, in the right only, or in both. So does a diff of two versions of a config. Any merge function is a function out of These, and getting all three cases right is the whole contract.

align [1, 2, 3, 4] ["a", "b"]. Gold: both (the zip). Coral: left only. Read the top row of every cell and you get [1, 2, 3, 4] back; read the bottom row and you get ["a", "b"].

Any two partial maps f and g out of X factor through A ✳ B in exactly one way. The projections are partial: "fst" has nothing to say about a B-only value.

A ✳ B is the product in the category of partial functions

A partial function from X to A is a function X → Option A: it may answer "nothing". With these as the arrows (the category is called Par), the product of A and B is not A × B. It is A ✳ B. Given f: X → A and g: X → B, pair them: both answer gives "both", one answer gives "this" or "that", neither gives nothing. Lean proves the projections come out right and that this pairing is the only one.

The empty set is both the start and the end object of Par (a zero object), and it is the unit of ✳. The coproduct in Par is still plain disjoint union. So Par has + for its sum and ✳ for its product, the way ordinary sets have + and ×. Turning partial maps into total maps by adding a base point (Option) is the textbook equivalence of Par with pointed sets, and it carries ✳ to ×.

The dial, with k tags. Put a tag from a k-element set on the "both" corner: A + B + k·AB. The tagged Option, 1 + k·A, turns that into a product too, 1 + k(A ✳ₖ B) ≅ (1 + kA)(1 + kB). That is the dial's lift 1 + kx made of sets. It works for every k ≥ 0 and for no negative k, since sets can't be negative.

Associativity is the mult-add cube

(A ✳ B) ✳ C and A ✳ (B ✳ C) both list the seven non-empty corners of a cube: A, B, C, AB, AC, BC, ABC. Lean writes the re-bracketing out case by case and checks both round trips; the checks confirm the pentagon and hexagon rules. So (finite sets, ✳, ∅) is a symmetric monoidal category, and Option is a strong monoidal functor into (sets, ×, one point): it sends ✳ to ×, and ∅ to the one-point set.

Counting sizes, the decategorification, sends ✳ to mult-add: |A ✳ B| = |A| ✳ |B|, and |Option A| = 1 + |A| is the lift. Nothing here is new; it is the reason mult-add's algebra is so well behaved. The cube's empty corner is the base point that the lift adds and the subtraction takes away.

The cube for A, B, C. The hollow corner is "nothing from any of them", the base point. The other seven are A ✳ B ✳ C.

Reduced Euler characteristic χ̃ = χ − 1, the lift read backwards. A point sits at 0, the identity. The circle sits on the wall: anything times a circle lands back at −1.

For spaces the same split holds up to deformation, and the circle is the wall

For pointed spaces, the product splits after one suspension (Σ, "stack the space between two poles"):

Σ(X × Y) ≃ ΣX ∨ ΣY ∨ Σ(X ∧ Y)

wedge plus smash, the same a + b + ab, now as shapes. This is a standard fact of homotopy theory. The checks compute the homology of a triangulated torus and its suspension and find exactly the pieces of S² ∨ S² ∨ S³.

Count each space by its reduced Euler characteristic χ̃ = χ − 1. Products multiply χ, so χ̃(X × Y) = χ̃X ✳ χ̃Y: χ̃ is the mult-add coordinate and χ is its lift. Lean proves this for cell counts at every t, not only t = −1. Spheres sit at ±1, and the circle, at χ̃ = −1, is mult-add's wall: S¹ × Y always has χ̃ = −1. The logic page found the same wall in sets, as the open interval.

The topology page already reads the torus's holes as the Pascal cube; this is the same cube with the base point taken out.

Lead worth following?

Not as mathematics; yes as a design tool. Everything here is textbook. Its value for the project is a firm underpinning: mult-add is a genuine categorical product, the lift is a functor, and the wall is the circle. So the framework's algebra rests on structure nobody disputes.

The useful lead is in software. These is the type of every merge, sync, diff and outer join, and the monoidal laws are exactly what make merges compose: merging three sources in either order gives the same seven cases. Treating "merge" as a function out of A ✳ B, and checking it against the Lean laws here, is a concrete way to keep merge code honest and loosely coupled.

The one open-ended question: negative dial values (OR at −1, XOR at −2) have no set model, only Euler-characteristic ones. Whether some category of signed or virtual objects has ✳₋₁ as its product is a fair question, but it is probably the known story of Euler characteristic and Mayer–Vietoris in other clothes.
