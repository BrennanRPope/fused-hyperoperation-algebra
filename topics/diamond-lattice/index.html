Square, then square again: the diamond lattice is two multiplications, and its critical slope is always 4/(1 + t)²

The diamond lattice is built by replacing every bond with a little diamond: two bonds in series, twice, in parallel. In the transmissivity t of a Potts bond (Q colours), series multiplies t and parallel is the landscape law FQ−2, 1−Q, whose lift multiplies e−K. So one step squares in t, then squares in e−K. The colour count Q only moves one wall. Slide Q and watch the frontier between hot and cold, the set where the complex-temperature (Fisher) zeros pile up, bend and break.

The four walls on the real line of t. Series multiplies t, so its walls are 0 and ∞ (∞ drawn at the right edge). Parallel multiplies e−K, so its walls are 1 (a perfect bond) and 1/(1 − Q) (the colouring bond, v = −1). Only that last wall moves with Q. It crosses ∞ at Q = 1 (the map turns into a polynomial), meets 1 at Q = 0 (the walls merge), and creeps towards 0 as Q grows.

One step is two squarings in two coordinates

T(t) = FQ−2,1−Q(t², t²) = (2t² + (Q−2)t⁴) / (1 + (Q−1)t⁴)

Series: two bonds in a row multiply transmissivity, t₁t₂. Parallel: couplings add, so 1 + v multiplies (mult-add on v), and in t that is the landscape law FQ−2,1−Q. Its lift is φ(t) = (1 − t)/(1 + (Q − 1)t), which is exactly 1/(1 + v) = e−K. So

φ(T(t)) = φ(t²)²

square in t, then square in φ. Swap t = (x − 1)/(x + Q − 1) and this is the classical map x ↦ ((x² + Q − 1)/(2x + Q − 2))² of Berker and Ostlund, x = eK. Brute-force sums over all colourings of the 4-bond and 16-bond diamonds confirm it.

The bend's sign never flips, because p² − 4q = Q²

On the landscape, a law is a "line group" when its two walls are real and apart, a "circle group" when they are complex, and on the border when they touch. For the Potts parallel law p = Q − 2 and q = 1 − Q, so

p² − 4q = (Q − 2)² + 4(Q − 1) = Q²

a perfect square. The walls are 1 and 1/(1 − Q), always real, and they meet only at Q = 0. So for real Q the circle type never happens and the sign tells the dynamics nothing. What matters is the zero: at Q = 0 the cold fixed point t = 1 stops being superattracting (slope 0) and turns parabolic (slope 1), T(t) − t = −t(t − 1)²/(1 + t²). The bend has one other job: q = 1 − Q is the reciprocal of the moving wall.

Every fixed point lies on one cubic curve, Q = (y − 1)²(y + 1)

Fixed points of T besides 0 and 1 are t = 1/y, where y solves Q = (y − 1)²(y + 1), and then eK = y². The horizontal line is your Q. Dots: fixed points, filled when attracting. The curve peaks at y = −1/3 with height 32/27: below that height a second branch gives an attracting antiferromagnetic point, which dies in a fold at Q = 32/27.

The slope there is always 4/(1 + t)²

T′(t*) = (2y/(y + 1))² = 4/(1 + t*)²

At any nontrivial fixed point the derivative of the map collapses to this. So the length exponent ν = ln 2 / ln T′(t*) is as closed-form as t* itself, and t* comes from one cubic. It is rational exactly when y is: Q = (m − 1)²(m + 1).

Beraha values: tested, and only Q = 3 stands out, for a plainer reason

The complex Q-plane, coloured by where the colouring point t = 1/(1 − Q) flows (amber hot, blue cold, green the antiferro point). Its frontier is where chromatic zeros pile up (Chio and Roeder; Chang, Roeder and Shrock). White ticks: Beraha values. Red tick: 32/27.

  • The critical cubic gets a root in the Beraha number field only for n = 3 and 6, that is Q = 1 and 3, both integers (all n up to 30).
  • On the real axis the colouring point switches basin at 3, 1.6389, 1.41, 1.32, ..., crowding onto 32/27. Of these only 3 is a Beraha value.
  • Why 3: T is even, and at Q = 3 the colouring wall −1/2 is the mirror of t* = 1/2, so it lands on t* in one step. The wall mirrors t* only when (y − 1)² = 1, y = 2, Q = 3.
  • For Q > 1 no level of the diamond has a real chromatic zero: the real orbit never reaches t = ∞.

OursThe parametrisation Q = (y − 1)²(y + 1), t* = 1/y, and the slope 4/(1 + t*)² at every fixed point (elementary; we did not find it written down). The mirror reason for Q = 3. The Beraha test.

Lead worth following?

No, this one closes. The landscape gives a clean way to say why the map looks the way it does (two squarings, one moving wall) and a tidy slope formula, but it predicts nothing the complex-dynamics papers don't already have. The Beraha question gets a clear negative: one hit (Q = 3) with a non-Beraha explanation, misses everywhere else. The bend's sign is idle because the discriminant is a perfect square. A reader who wants more should go to Chang, Roeder and Shrock, where the real-axis structure is worked out fully.
