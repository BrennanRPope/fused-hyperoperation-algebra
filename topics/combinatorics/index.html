The rung flow counts groups within groups

Multiply out (1 + a)(1 + b)(1 + c) − 1 and every nonempty subset appears once: the circle-operation cube is the oldest trick in counting. One rung up, the flow E(x) = eˣ − 1 wraps pieces into nonempty sets. Start from single points and climb: rung 1 counts sets, rung 2 set partitions, rung 3 partitions of partitions. Between whole rungs the numbers turn negative, so a fractional rung counts nothing.

Bar n is the number of structures on n labelled points at rung t (n! times the xⁿ coefficient of Eᵗ(x)), drawn on a log scale. Teal bars are counts; tomato bars are negative. Try t = 0.5 or 1.5.

One structure on 8 points at each whole rung. Rung 1: one loop, a set. Rung 2: loops of points, a set partition. Rung 3: loops of loops. Each rung puts a nonempty set around the pieces of the rung below: that is E.

The cube lists subsets, and the dial paints them

(1 + x₁)(1 + x₂)⋯(1 + xₙ) − 1 = x₁ ∘₁ x₂ ∘₁ ⋯ ∘₁ xₙ = Σ over nonempty S of x_S

Each corner of the n-cube is a subset; its layers are Pascal's rows, the tesseract's 1-4-6-4-1. Set every xᵢ = 1 and the count is 2ⁿ − 1.

Turn the dial: (1 + k·x₁)⋯(1 + k·xₙ) weighs a subset by k^|S|. For whole k that is a count too: each chosen element wears one of k colours, giving (1 + k)ⁿ = qⁿ in all. At k = −1 the signed corners cancel to 0, which is inclusion–exclusion (proved on the statistics page); it counts derangements 1, 0, 1, 2, 9, 44, 265. At k = −2 the total is (−1)ⁿ, parity. Weighting the corners by q to the sum of their positions gives the q-binomials, done on the q page.

The same cube sorts compositions: m numbers adding to n, each zero or a positive part. Pick the corner of nonzero parts, then split n among them. For two parts that is the pairs (a, b), either or both nonzero: n + 1 of them.

n! is a circle-operation fold, and the cube sorts permutations by cycles

1 ∘₁ 2 = 5, 5 ∘₁ 3 = 23, 23 ∘₁ 4 = 119, … : 1 ∘₁ 2 ∘₁ ⋯ ∘₁ (n − 1) = n! − 1

The lift says why: (1 + 1)(1 + 2)⋯(1 + (n − 1)) = n!. The picture says more. Build a permutation by placing the points one at a time. Point i + 1 either starts a new cycle (axis i off, weight 1) or goes right after one of the i points already placed (axis i on, weight i). Pick "Axis weights 1, 2, 3" on the cube above: its layers 1, 6, 11, 6 are the permutations of 4 points with 4, 3, 2 and 1 cycles, Stirling's cycle numbers.

With the dial, each permutation weighs k^(n − cycles), and the total is (1 + k)(1 + 2k)⋯. At k = −1 that weight is the permutation's sign, and the axis of weight 1 sits exactly on the dial's wall −1/k = 1. One entry on the wall sends the whole fold to the wall, so even and odd permutations balance.

The exponential formula is exactly one step of the flow

For labelled structures, (1 + A)(1 + B) − 1 counts the ways to split the points into an A-part and a B-part, either one possibly empty but not both. Combine C/n with itself n times by the circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0) and let n grow: the result is e^C − 1 = E(C), the same staircase that builds e. So E(C) counts nonempty sets of C-pieces.

Put connected graphs in as C (1, 1, 4, 38, 728 on 1 to 5 points) and E returns all graphs (1, 2, 8, 64, 1024). Going back, log(1 + x), is one rung down, E⁻¹. So yes: the exponential formula is the rung flow at t = 1, and nothing more exotic.

The dial version, n copies of C/n under ∘ₖ, tends to (e^(kC) − 1)/k, which weighs a structure by k for each piece beyond the first. Fed nonempty sets, its layers are the Stirling numbers S(n, j), set partitions by block count.

Sign of the count at rung t (across, 0 to 2) for n = 1 to 16 points (down). Tomato cells are negative, grey cells zero. The whole rungs t = 1 and t = 2 have no tomato cell; t = 0 is the bare point, zero beyond n = 1.

Between the rungs the counts go negative

The number of structures on n points at rung t is a polynomial in t (Jabotinsky, Comtet). For n = 3 it is t(3t − 1)/2: 1 at t = 1, 5 at t = 2, 12 at t = 3, and negative for t between 0 and 1/3. For n = 4 it is t(2t − 1)(3t − 1)/2, negative between 1/3 and 1/2.

The half rung is the series x + x²/4 + x³/48 + 0·x⁴ + x⁵/3840 − 7x⁶/92160 + ⋯. Done twice it gives eˣ − 1 exactly, but its x⁶ term is negative. Fractional rungs above 1 do no better: t = 3/2 stays positive up to 15 points and goes negative at 16. Baker (1958) showed these fractional series do not even converge.

So the rung ruler has a counting meaning only at whole steps, where it measures depth of nesting. One step below the ground, t = −1, the counts are the signed cyclic orders (−1)ⁿ⁻¹(n − 1)!, the Möbius function of the partition lattice (Rota 1964).

Lead worth following?

No, not inside combinatorics. Everything on this page is the standard generating-function toolkit seen through the lift, and the lift is exactly how combinatorialists already work: a product of (1 + x) is a choice of subset. The circle-operation words name these steps neatly but predict no new count.

One fact is worth carrying back to the rung work, because it is firm and it is about the flow itself: whole rungs of E have a concrete meaning (how many levels of grouping), while fractional rungs are only an interpolation. Their coefficients turn negative and their series diverge, so a fractional rung should never be read as "part of a grouping".
