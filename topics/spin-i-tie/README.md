## Verdict first

![The tie at a glance. Left column, why quantum needs i: shift every energy by c; the state turns by an extra common phase; that is invisible only if J commutes with everything, which gives one shared J with J squared = -1. Right column, the twist bubble: rotate the round twist field; the rotation is a constant gauge turn; angular momentum becomes the electric charge of that turn, so spinning needs charge. Bottom: the shared root.](figures/t1-the-tie-at-a-glance.png)

1. **Yes, there is a real tie, and it is a shared mechanism, not just a resemblance.** In both questions a symmetry acts only up to an internal circle turn (a U(1)), and its conserved quantity is handed to that circle:
   - Quantum side: shifting every energy by c is the same as an extra common phase turn generated by J. Energy is the charge of that turn (E = ħω).
   - Bubble side: turning space around the round twist field is the same as a constant colour turn W (check S4). By Gauss's law the bubble's angular momentum becomes the electric charge of that turn, so **a spinning bubble must carry charge**.
   - **That shared mechanism is textbook**, not ours: symmetries that act "up to a gauge turn" (Forgács–Manton 1980), and the angular momentum of a gauge field as a surface integral of its electric field (Volkov–Wöhnert 2003; van der Bij–Radu 2002).
2. **A sharper version is ours (a reading of textbook facts; I didn't find it in print):** *a constant shift is a convention exactly when its generator commutes with everything* (S1). The two questions use this one lemma in opposite directions:
   - why-i demands that the energy zero be a convention, and that forces one shared J with J² = −1: the i.
   - In the bubble, the zero of the electric potential (c₀) is *not* a convention, because the non-Abelian field doesn't commute with it. The staticity theorem says a regular bubble can only spin if that physical potential zero meets a charge (Brodbeck–Heusler–Straumann–Volkov 1997).
3. **The law's f = 1 is exactly the "one shared i" case (ours, computed in S3).** Along the law's twist line, count the quarter turns whose constant shift stays a convention: **3 at the two vacua (f = 0, 2), exactly 1 at the law's f = 1, 0 everywhere else.** That is the same 0 / 1 / 3 pattern as real / complex / quaternionic quantum mechanics on the why-i page. At f = 1 the round field is the Wu–Yang monopole, and its one surviving quarter turn is the hedgehog J(x) = 2x̂·T, with J² = −1.
4. **Rotation, phase and charge meet in one textbook law.** Quantizing rotation (J = n·ħ/2, Saha 1936, Wilson 1949) and demanding a single-valued complex phase (Dirac 1931) give the same charge lattice (S5). A spin that follows a direction over the sphere carries a unit monopole that real amplitudes cannot carry (S7). So "rotation forces a circle phase" is true, and it was already known.
5. **Toward quantum gravity, the tie lands on the one spot where quantum mechanics and gravity disagree: the zero of energy.** why-i's lever is "a constant energy changes nothing" (its assumption A4). Gravity breaks exactly that: a constant energy gravitates and sets the expansion (S6). In the law, one fused shift a ↦ 1 + k·a carries both: an imaginary k turns the state (invisible), a real k = √(Λ/3) grows the universe (visible, round 16). That is the cosmological constant problem seen through the law. It is **not solved** here, and **no new number comes out**.

**Does it open a lead?** Partly. The tie is genuine and worth keeping as a map, but its working parts are textbook. One thread is worth a test because it is where the derivation of i meets quantum gravity directly: in canonical quantum gravity the Hamiltonian is a constraint (Hψ = 0), so there is no energy zero to shift. why-i's lever is missing there, and the best-known state of the universe, the Hartle–Hawking state, is real-valued. Complex phases come back only once a clock emerges semiclassically. A test: does why-i's proof go through with "a clock emerges" in place of "the energy zero is a convention"? That is known territory (the problem of time), so judge any result against it.

**Prediction candidate for round 27:** none from the law. The textbook chain gives a conditional one (section 6): *if* a regular spinning f = 1 bubble exists, its frame dragging and its electric charge rise together in a fixed ratio. That is GR plus Yang–Mills, not a place where the law and the textbook give different numbers.

**The spin dispute, only as far as the tie needs it (section 5):** Volkov, who wrote the 1997 "no" paper with Straumann, sided with the "yes" by 1998. The slowly spinning bubble exists to first order and must be charged. Whether an exact spinning bubble exists is open, and van der Bij–Radu (2002) conjecture it doesn't. The tie holds either way, because the charge requirement holds for any regular spinning solution.

## 1. The shift lemma: one algebraic fact under both questions

![Panel A: how visible a constant shift is against its size. With the shared J as the shift's generator the difference stays at machine zero; with a generator that does not commute it jumps to order one. Panel B: four boxes. Quantum: demanding the energy zero be a convention makes J central and gives the shared i. Twist bubble: the potential zero c0 times tau_z does not commute with the non-Abelian field, so c0 is physical. Staticity theorem: the bubble is static unless Tr(phi at infinity Q) is nonzero, so to spin it needs a physical potential zero and a charge. Bottom left: same algebra, contrapositive.](figures/t2-the-shift-lemma.png)

**The lemma.** Add c·G to a generator H. The result is the old motion followed by a separate turn by G, e^(t(H + cG)) = e^(tH)·e^(tcG), for every t and c, exactly when G commutes with H (S1). Then no expectation value moves. If G doesn't commute, the shift is visible.

- **Quantum side (why-i).** On complex quantum mechanics, written in real numbers, the shared J commutes with every observable. Shifting all energies changes nothing: the largest difference is 7.4e-14, and expectation values move by 7.3e-14. A rotation generator that doesn't commute moves things by at least 0.22. On real quantum mechanics (ℝ⁴, every symmetric matrix observable), the only thing that commutes with all observables is the identity (commutant of dimension 1), so there is no quarter turn to carry the shift. That is why-i's reason real QM fails A4.
- **Bubble side (BHSV 1997).** A constant electric potential φ gives an electric field E = −Dφ = −[A, φ]. In an Abelian theory that is always zero, and the paper says so: there c₀ "has no physical significance". In the non-Abelian bubble the constant term enters the field strength "by virtue of the covariant derivative", so c₀ is physical. Their staticity theorem, Ω_H J − Tr{φ_∞ Q} = 0 ⇒ static, has no horizon term for a soliton. So a regular bubble can only spin if Tr{φ_∞ Q} ≠ 0: the potential zero must be physical *and* the bubble must be charged.

**The tie, in one line (ours):** why-i says "a constant shift that changes nothing needs a shared, commuting quarter turn". The bubble shows the other side: where the shift's generator doesn't commute, the potential zero becomes physical, and that is exactly the room the bubble needs to spin. BHSV wrote that they "lack a deeper physical understanding" of why rotation needs charge. The surface-integral papers of 2002–2003 (section 3) supplied the mechanism. The shift-lemma reading is mine, and I didn't find it stated.

**Through the law (S2, exact):** two mult-add steps with the same dial never twist; with dials k₁ and k₂ the twist is (k₁ − k₂)·a·b. "Commuting" is the law's "no twist". why-i's shared J is the case of one shared dial. The non-Abelian bubble is the twisted case.

## 2. At the law's f = 1, exactly one quarter turn survives

![Panel A: along the law's twist line f from 0 to 2, how visible a constant shift along the hedgehog direction is (a V shape, zero at f = 1) and the number of generators whose constant shift is a convention (3 at f = 0 and 2, 1 at f = 1, 0 elsewhere). Panel B: bars comparing why-i's count of shared quarter turns (real 0, complex 1, quaternionic 3) with the twist line's surviving quarter turns (vacua 3, the law 1, other 0).](figures/t3-one-quarter-turn-survives-at-f1.png)

Round 25 placed the law's f = 1 on the weak force's round twist field, with f = 1 − w in the standard notation Aᵢ = εₐᵢⱼ xⱼ (1 − w)/r² Tₐ. Bartnik–McKinnon's bubble passes through w = 0, the law's f = 1, at its node.

S3 counts, at a point, the colour generators that can stay covariantly constant (so that a constant potential shift along them stays a convention). It uses the curvature and its first covariant derivatives:

| Where on the twist line | Count | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| f = 0 (w = 1, empty vacuum) | 3 | all three turns, like quaternions' i, j, k |
| **f = 1 (w = 0, the law)** | **1** | **one quarter turn: the hedgehog J(x) = 2x̂·T, J² = −1** |
| f = 2 (w = −1, pure gauge) | 3 | a vacuum again |
| f = 0.6 held fixed, and a bubble-shaped profile at r = 0.8 | 0 | no shift is a convention |

The hedgehog's covariant derivative is 7.8e-11 at f = 1 and proportional to |w| elsewhere (ratio spread 3.2e-11).

- **Textbook:** the w = 0 field is the Wu–Yang monopole (Wu and Yang 1969, 1975). Picking out one U(1) along the hedgehog direction is 't Hooft's "Abelian projection" (1974).
- **Ours:** the count along the law's twist line, and the match with why-i's 0 / 1 / 3. **The law's f = 1 is the one place on the twist line where the field leaves a single shared i**, which is the structure why-i says quantum mechanics needs. This is a structural match. It does not derive quantum mechanics from the twist field.

## 3. Turning the bubble is a colour turn, so spin is charge (textbook)

![Panel A: the T3 part of the round twist field in the z = 0 plane, circulating arrows. Turning space about z equals turning colours by minus T3, residual 1.8e-10 against a field change of at least 0.06. Panel B: a bubble inside a far sphere with arrows of electric flux. J equals the flux of W dotted with E through the far sphere. A static purely magnetic bubble has J = 0; a spinning one needs electric flux Q with J over Q fixed by the winding.](figures/t4-rotation-is-a-gauge-turn.png)

- **S4:** for any profile w(r), the Lie derivative of the round field along a rotation about z equals a constant gauge turn W = −T₃ (residual 1.8e-10 against a field change of at least 0.06). This is the field being "spherically symmetric up to a gauge turn" (Forgács–Manton 1980).
- Then J_z = ∫⟨E, L_ξA⟩ = ∫⟨E, D W⟩ = ∮⟨W, E⟩·dS, using ad-invariance (checked to 1.8e-15) and Gauss's law D·E = 0. **The angular momentum is the electric flux of the compensating U(1).** A static, purely magnetic bubble (E = 0) carries none, which is round 26's finding that rigid spin of a round field is a pure gauge turn.
- **Textbook:** Volkov and Wöhnert (2003) write J = −∮⟨(A_φ − W_φ) F^{k0}⟩ dS_k. Van der Bij and Radu (2002) derive J ∝ Q in general and get J/Q = 4πn in their units, for winding n.

## 4. Rotation, phase and charge give one law (textbook)

![Panel A: field angular momentum of a charge beside a monopole at three separations, all on the dashed line eg/4pi. Panel B: allowed values of eg from quantized rotation (orange) and from a single-valued complex phase (blue); the dots line up. Panel C: a sphere with arrows: a spin that follows the direction over the sphere carries Chern number -1, a unit monopole; real amplitudes can't.](figures/t5-rotation-phase-charge.png)

- **S5:** a charge e = 1.3 beside a monopole g = 0.7 stores angular momentum 0.072403 in the field at separations 0.5, 1 and 2. That is eg/4π = 0.072415 to 1.7e-4, the error coming from the finite integration cutoff (Thomson 1904).
- Rotation's rule J = n·ħ/2 (Saha 1936; Wilson 1949) and the single-valued phase e^(ieg/ħ) (Dirac 1931) give **the same allowed eg**: 2π, 4π, 6π, 8π, 10π (ħ = 1).
- **S7:** a spin-½ that follows a direction over the whole sphere carries Chern number −1, a unit monopole (Berry 1984), with a gap of 2. With real amplitudes only (no σ_y), a direction-following family must close its gap (gap 1.7e-16 at one point). A real family that stays gapped has Chern number 0, and loop phases can only be 0 or π.

So the hunch "a rotation forces a circle-shaped phase" holds, in the precise textbook sense: **quantized rotation of a charge–monopole pair is the same statement as a single-valued complex phase**, and the law's f = 1 field is a monopole of exactly this kind. All of this is known.

## 5. The spin dispute, as far as the tie needs it

- **"Yes" (Brodbeck, Heusler, Straumann and Volkov, PRL 79, 4310 (1997), gr-qc/9707057):** slowly spinning, globally regular excitations of the Bartnik–McKinnon bubble exist and carry electric charge, although the bubble at rest is neutral. Pure Einstein–Yang–Mills has only massless fields, which allows a slower polynomial fall-off and "an additional degree of freedom". Counting solutions (four at infinity, three at the origin, six in all) leaves at least a one-parameter family.
- **"No" (Volkov and Straumann, PRL 79, 1428 (1997), hep-th/9704026):** the spinning *black holes* exist and must be charged, but the bubble admits no regular slow spin.
- **Later:** the review by Volkov and Gal'tsov (hep-th/9810070, 1998) says these particles are "the only known example of solitons, whether gravitating or not, which admit regular, slowly rotating generalizations". So Volkov sided with "yes" within a year. Van der Bij and Radu (gr-qc/0111046) note the perturbative solutions exist, say "it is not clear whether they can be extended to exact solutions", and conjecture that exact rotating regular solutions don't exist.
- **For the tie this is enough.** The charge requirement holds for any regular spinning solution, perturbative or exact, through the staticity theorem and the surface integral. Existence of an exact spinning bubble stays open. I didn't re-derive BHSV's equations.

## 6. Toward quantum gravity: how far the tie gets, and where it stops

![Panel A: a transition probability against a constant added to every energy, a flat line. Panel B: the size of the universe against time for three vacuum energies; larger vacuum energy grows faster. Panel C: the fused shift (1 + k tau)^n in the complex plane: imaginary k walks round the unit circle, real k walks out along the real axis.](figures/t6-quantum-gravity-edge.png)

**What the tie reaches.**
- **Quantum to gauge:** fully, and textbook (sections 3–4). Rotation, the complex phase and charge are one structure.
- **Gauge to gravity:** the bubble exists only because of gravity (pure Yang–Mills has no static bubbles), and a spinning bubble drags frames by 2J/r³ outside (round 26). Through J ∝ Q, frame dragging and charge are locked. That is classical GR plus Yang–Mills.
- **Quantum to gravity, the real meeting point:** why-i's i rests on A4, "a constant energy changes nothing". S6 shows the clash in one place:
  - Quantum: adding up to 300 to every energy moves transition probabilities by 3.0e-14.
  - Gravity: the same vacuum energy ρ sets the expansion: a(t = 2) = 1.784, 3.183, 10.130 for ρ = 0.01, 0.04, 0.16 (G = 1).
  - The law: one fused shift (1 + kτ)ᴺ reproduces both, with k real = √(8πρ/3) giving 1.784, 3.183, 10.130 and k = −iρ giving modulus 1.0000000 (to 3e-7).
  - So **the energy zero that quantum mechanics may shift freely is the energy that gravity weighs.** That is the cosmological constant problem (Weinberg 1989). Round 16 already found that whether a constant grows (cosh) or turns (cos) is set by the velocity dial's sign, k² = −q_v·q_s. That is the Wick rotation, which is textbook.

**Where it stops.**
- Nothing here fixes ħ, Λ, or the sign choice between turning and growing. No new number comes out.
- **The derivation of i needs a free energy zero, and quantum gravity may not have one.** In canonical quantum gravity the Hamiltonian is a constraint (Hψ = 0), so there's no zero to shift. The Hartle–Hawking state is real-valued, and complex phases appear with the semiclassical clock (Halliwell, *Introductory Lectures on Quantum Cosmology*, arXiv:0909.2566). That is the honest frontier: **why-i explains i wherever there is a clock, and quantum gravity is exactly where the clock is in question.** This is the one test I'd suggest (see Verdict).
- A gravitational version of Dirac's argument exists: a gravitomagnetic monopole (the NUT parameter) would quantize mass and energy (Dowker; Zee, PRL 55, 2379 (1985); summarized in Lynden-Bell and Nouri-Zonoz, gr-qc/9612049). It ties energy, the charge of why-i's J, to a gravitational phase. NUT space has closed timelike curves, and no such monopole is known. I cite it only as the nearest published bridge, and didn't check it in detail.

**Conditional prediction (textbook, not the law's):** *if* a regular spinning f = 1 bubble exists, its outside frame dragging (2J/r³) and its electric charge Q rise together with J/Q fixed by its winding (van der Bij–Radu). If J comes in steps of ħ/2, so do the drag and the charge. This doesn't separate the law from textbook physics, so it doesn't count as a prediction of the law.

## Assumptions

- Units: ħ = 1, Heaviside–Lorentz for S5 (charge e at the origin, monopole g at distance d; integration out to 4000·d), G = 1 for S6.
- S3 counts generators that commute with the curvature and its first covariant derivatives at one point (a necessary condition for a covariantly constant field), and checks the hedgehog's covariant derivative directly. "f = 0.6 held fixed" is a test configuration, not a solution. The bubble-shaped profile w = (1 − r²)/(1 + r²) is illustrative, not the Bartnik–McKinnon solution.
- S4 uses flat space and the regular gauge. In curved space the same identity holds with the Killing vector of the rotation.
- S6 uses flat Friedmann expansion with vacuum energy only. Round 16's closed slices change the curve's shape but not the point.
- Literature: the four 1997–2003 spin papers and the 1998 review were read from their arXiv abstracts and text excerpts. Older citations (Dirac, Saha, Wilson, Thomson, Wu–Yang, 't Hooft, Forgács–Manton, Berry, Weinberg, Dowker, Hartle–Hawking) are textbook and were not re-fetched. Zee 1985 was checked only from its abstract.
