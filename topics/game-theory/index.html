When winnings multiply, the dial keeps the books

In repeated play, fortunes grow by factors: a learner's trust in an advisor, a bettor's bankroll, the odds between rated players. Each factor is a lift 1 + k·x, so the gains themselves combine by the circle operation x ∘ₖ y = x + y + k·xy. Read that way, the multiplicative weights algorithm, Kelly betting and Elo ratings are one picture, and a family of cooperative games gets a fair split in closed form. Nearly all of it is classical; the page shows it through the lift.

The learning rate is the dial, and the regret bound is the lift

Multiplicative weights (Littlestone and Warmuth 1994; Hedge, Freund and Schapire 1997) multiplies each advisor's weight by 1 + ε·g after a gain g. Advisor i's weight is then a lift of its own dial fold:

∏(1 + ε·gₜ) = 1 + ε·(g₁ ∘_ε g₂ ∘_ε … ∘_ε g_T)

The total weight obeys the same law with the learner's own expected gain rₜ (Lean: hedge_step), so it equals n·∏(1 + ε·rₜ). One advisor's weight is part of the total, so the learner's lifted fortune is at least the leader's divided by n (lifted_regret, proved for two advisors; checked for up to ten). Logs turn that into Σ log(1 + εr) ≥ Σ log(1 + εg) − log n.

What the corners cost

The lifted bound is free. The price is paid only when you go back to plain gains, where each round's log(1 + εg) sits between εg − (εg)² and εg. The squared term is the corner. Adding them up gives the textbook regret bound

regret ≤ log n / ε + ε·T

and with ε = √(log n / T), regret ≤ 2√(T log n). All three held in 300 simulated runs, including advisors that take turns being good. Small ε means small corners but a wide band; large ε, the reverse. The slider shows the trade.

Kelly betting: bets compose by the dial at k = f, and ruin is the wall

Stake a fraction f of your bankroll on a bet that returns r. Your wealth multiplies by 1 + f·r, so a run of bets gives 1 + f·(r₁ ∘_f r₂ ∘_f …): the fraction you bet is the dial (kelly_many). The dial's wall is r = −1/f, losing the whole stake, and once you hit it nothing afterwards helps (kelly_ruin). That wall is why the log matters: log(1 + f·r) runs to minus infinity there.

Kelly (1956) maximised the growth rate E log(1 + f·r). For a bet paying b to 1 that you win with chance p, the best fraction is f* = p − (1 − p)/b. At f* the lift has a clean meaning: a win multiplies your wealth by p/π and a loss by (1 − p)/(1 − π), where π = 1/(1 + b) is the bookmaker's implied chance (kelly_ratio). You grow by betting your beliefs against theirs, and the growth rate is the relative entropy between them. Bet twice Kelly and the growth falls to about zero; past that it turns negative and you shrink, even with the odds on your side.

Elo: a rating gap is a rapidity

Elo's model (Elo 1978, the logistic form of Bradley and Terry 1952, first in Zermelo 1929) gives a player rated d points higher odds of 10^(d/400). Gaps add along a chain, so odds multiply, and odds − 1 combines by the circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0) (odds_circ). The interesting ruler is the edge s = 2P − 1, the win chance measured from a coin flip. It composes exactly like relativistic speeds (edge_compose):

s(A over C) = (s₁ + s₂) / (1 + s₁·s₂)

That is the landscape point (0, −1). The dictionary is complete: the rating gap is rapidity (s = tanh(gap · ln 10 / 800)), the odds are the Doppler lift (1 + s)/(1 − s) (odds_is_lift), and certainty is the speed of light. A 200-point favourite wins 76%, an edge of 0.52. Two such gaps compose to an edge of 0.82, a 91% favourite; simply adding the edges would claim 1.04, a 102% chance. A 2000-point favourite still loses about once in 100,000 games.

The dial game: each corner of the cube is shared equally

Give player i a stand-alone value xᵢ and let a coalition be worth the dial fold of its members, v(S) = (∏(1 + k·xᵢ) − 1)/k. Disjoint groups combine by v(S ∪ T) = v(S) + v(T) + k·v(S)·v(T) (dial_game_union): k > 0 is synergy, k < 0 overlap, and k = −1 is "at least one of us succeeds". Normalised to v(all) = 1 this is Sugeno's λ-measure (1974).

Expanding the product lays the worth out on the cube: each coalition S is a corner carrying k^(|S|−1)·∏xᵢ, and these corners are exactly the game's Harsanyi dividends. Shapley's value gives every dividend to its members in equal shares (Harsanyi 1963), so it comes out in closed form:

φᵢ = xᵢ · Σₘ kᵐ·eₘ(others)/(m + 1) = xᵢ ∫₀¹ ∏ⱼ≠ᵢ (1 + k·t·xⱼ) dt

Checked exactly against all orderings for up to six players; proved in Lean for two and three (shapley3_eq, shapley3_efficient). The integral is Owen's diagonal formula, and Owen's multilinear extension, everyone showing up with chance t, is just the dial fold of t·xᵢ (multilinear3). At t = ½ it gives the Banzhaf value in one product: βᵢ = xᵢ·∏ⱼ≠ᵢ(1 + k·xⱼ/2) (banzhaf3_eq).

One consequence reads straight off the cube: your bonus ratio φᵢ/xᵢ depends on your partners, not on you. With synergy the small player earns the biggest ratio (k = 2: 1.44, 1.67, 2.00); with overlap the big player keeps the most (k = −1: 0.81, 0.72, 0.65). At k = −1 this split is the actuarial page's uniform-decrement split of competing causes.

Lead worth following?

Not as mathematics; yes, a small one, as a design tool. Every proof here was already a log-of-a-product proof, so the lift explains rather than extends them. The dial game's closed form is tidy but sits inside a well-worked theory of fuzzy measures.

The useful part is practical. The dial game is a one-knob model of combos: set k and you have said exactly how much a group is worth beyond its members, and the Shapley formula says how to credit each member fairly, with the cube showing where every point came from. For anyone scoring team play or card combos, that is a ready-made, checkable rule. Kelly's wall and the Elo dictionary are good explanations to keep, but they predict nothing new.
