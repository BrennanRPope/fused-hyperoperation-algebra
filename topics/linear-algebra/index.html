Linear algebra is the mult-add cube, one dimension up

The determinant of I + A is the mult-add cube: 1 + (λ₁ ⊞ λ₂ ⊞ … ⊞ λₙ), with the principal minors as its corners, counted 1, 3, 3, 1 in three dimensions. Almost everything here is known mathematics seen through the shift x ↦ 1 + x. The strongest find is that your soliton cube is exactly a determinant det(I + M).

The unit cube stretched by 1 + a, 1 + b, 1 + c along three eigen-directions. Its volume det(I + A) is the unit cube, 3 slabs (the trace), 3 beams (the 2×2 minors) and 1 corner block (the determinant).

The determinant is the cube standard

Expand det(I + A) and every term is a principal minor: pick a set S of rows, take the same columns, and multiply out. That is the corner expansion of the mult-add cube, with a minor sitting at each corner.

det(I + kA) = 1 + k·e₁ + k²·e₂ + k³·e₃

e₁ is the trace, e₂ the sum of the three 2×2 principal minors, e₃ the determinant. When the eigenvalues sit on the diagonal (a triangular matrix), this is exactly the cube of the eigenvalues:

det(I + A) − 1 = λ₁ ⊞ λ₂ ⊞ λ₃

So the trace adds the eigenvalues, the determinant multiplies them, and the shifted determinant mult-adds them. A matrix sits on the wall, det(I + A) = 0, exactly when −1 is one of its eigenvalues.

Known since Fredholm (1903), who defined det(I + K) for integral operators by this same corner sum, now called the Fredholm determinant. It is the infinite mult-add of the eigenvalues.

One formula covers the whole dial reframing

Write D_k(A) = e₁ + k·e₂ + k²·e₃, so that det(I + kA) = 1 + k·D_k(A). Then for every weight k:

D_k(A ⊞ₖ B) = D_k(A) ⊞ₖ D_k(B)

At k = 0 this says the trace is additive. At k = 1 it says det(I + A) is multiplicative. They are one law at two settings of your dial, and Lean proves it for 2×2 matrices over any ring and 3×3 over any field.

Your linearizer L(x) = log(1 + x) carries one end to the other: log det(I + A) = tr log(I + A), and det(e^Y) = e^(tr Y).

The content is textbook (Jacobi's formula, log-det). Writing trace and determinant as two dial settings of a single homomorphism is the reframing.

Vectors and maps: two readings

Shift the field. standard The fused ring (u + v + 1, mult-add) is the ordinary ring moved by one, so its vector spaces are ordinary ones with the origin at the wall corner (−1, −1). A fused-linear map is an affine map that fixes that corner. Mult-adding a is zooming by 1 + a about the wall, as drawn.

Use mult-add as the addition. standard On (−1, ∞)ⁿ, with ⊞ as addition and the mult-add power x^(⊞t) = (1 + x)^t − 1 as scaling, you get a real vector space. log(1 + x) maps it onto ordinary ℝⁿ. Its linear maps are monomial maps, 1 + fᵢ = ∏ (1 + xⱼ)^mᵢⱼ. A matrix becomes a table of exponents, and composing two maps multiplies their tables. This is toric geometry's view, and Aitchison's geometry of proportions.

Matrices under mult-add standard

A ⊞ B = A + B + AB,   I + (A ⊞ B) = (I + A)(I + B)

Order now matters, because matrices don't commute. Off the wall every matrix has a way back, (I + A)⁻¹ − I. Mult-add subtraction X ⊟ A = (X − A)(I + A)⁻¹ undoes ⊞, just as it does for rates.

The Cayley transform, which turns a skew matrix S into a rotation, is mult-add subtraction of S from its own transpose:

(I + S)⁻¹(I − S) = I + (Sᵀ ⊟ S)

Matrix mult-add is Jacobson's circle operation (1945), and the group of I + A is the adjoint group of the ring. The Cayley transform dates from 1846. Writing it as Sᵀ ⊟ S is a reframing I haven't seen stated.

Rotations come from the dial reframing

I + kX is a rotation exactly when Xᵀ ⊞ₖ X = 0. For a 2×2 X = [[x, −y], [y, x]] this reads 2x + k(x² + y²) = 0: a circle through 0, centred on the weighted wall −1/k.

At k = 1 the circle sits on the wall −1, the rotations shifted by one. As k shrinks the circles grow and flatten into the vertical axis. At k = 0 the corner drops out and the condition is Xᵀ + X = 0, the skew matrices. That is the Lie algebra: the group flattens to its tangent line at the turn of the dial.

Off-diagonal entries turn the dial reframing

For a 2×2 matrix M:

det(I + M) − 1 = m₁₁ ⊞_w m₂₂,   w = 1 − m₁₂m₂₁ / (m₁₁m₂₂)

With no coupling (diagonal) w = 1, plain mult-add, volume. A rank-one matrix gives w = 0, plain addition. Coupling the two directions slides the weight between them, so the corner term is how much the directions still act independently.

An elementary identity. The point is the reading: the dial weight is not a free choice, it is the coupling ratio of a matrix.

Tensor and exterior powers on the dial known

The cube's corners are the exterior powers: det(I + A) = Σ tr Λʲ(A), the trace of A acting on the whole exterior algebra. The symmetric powers give the inverse of the mirror image, Σ tr Sʲ(A) = 1 / det(I − A). Put both on your dial as det(I + kA)^(1/k):

k = 1det(I + A): exterior powers, fermions, volume
k = 0e^(tr A): the classical limit, plain addition
k = −11 / det(I − A): symmetric powers, bosons
k = −2det(I − 2A)^(−½): the real Gaussian integral

This is Vere-Jones's α-permanent family (1988) with α = −k: the determinant and the permanent are two settings of one dial. Each eigenvalue contributes (1 + kλ)^(1/k), which is the Tsallis q-exponential with q = 1 − k, the same link the thermodynamics page found. The k = −2 setting matching your parity rung is a pattern I noticed, not an established connection.

Lead: the soliton cube is a determinant lead

Hirota's three-soliton τ, your weighted mult-add cube, is det(I + M) for the Cauchy-type matrix Mᵢⱼ = 2√(kᵢkⱼ)/(kᵢ + kⱼ)·e^((ηᵢ+ηⱼ)/2).

Each principal minor of a Cauchy matrix factors into pair weights ((kᵢ − kⱼ)/(kᵢ + kⱼ))², so the corner expansion of the determinant is the pairwise weighted cube. A generic matrix does not factor that way: its triple corner is not the product of its pair weights. So the Cauchy matrix is what makes the soliton cube pairwise.

Known since the 1980s (Sato's τ functions; Pöppe 1984). What it gives you is the reason the cube appears. A next step would be to test whether other equations with determinant τ functions (KP, sine-Gordon, Toda) have their weights on one k-dial.

Turning the dial

Every nonzero real k is the same law in other units, so the real dial has only three different places: 0 (trace), anywhere else (det(I + A), rescaled), and ∞ (the bare determinant). The new ground opens when the dial weight stops being a real number. Put a 2×2 matrix on the dial and your whole landscape plane appears, with its parabola, its walls and its three countries.

The walls are the eigenvalues standard

Read det(I + kA) as a function of the dial. It is the characteristic polynomial run backwards, det(I + kA) = (−k)ⁿ·p_A(−1/k), so it crosses zero exactly where the wall −1/k lands on an eigenvalue. Turning the dial slides the wall along the number line, and the matrix goes singular each time the wall passes one of its eigenvalues.

At k = −1 it is det(I − A) = p_A(1). Fredholm's own parameter, det(I − λK), is this dial with λ = −k.

Drawn for eigenvalues 2, ½ and −1: zeros at k = −½, −2 and 1.

Only the ends of the real dial differ reframing

k(A ⊞ₖ B) = (kA) ⊞ (kB), so any nonzero k, fractional, negative or large, is the k = 1 law measured in units of k. In the box picture k stretches every side to k·λ. Small k leaves a thin box made almost entirely of slabs, which is the trace. Large k makes the corner block swamp everything, which is the determinant. The cube is the bridge between the two.

k = −1 is not a new law either. It is the same law reflected, which is why the earlier pages found failure union as mult-add's mirror image.

Complex k turns the circles standard

With a complex weight, I + kz has length 1 on a circle through 0 centred at the wall −1/k. The size of k sets the circle's size and its angle turns the circle around 0, so the complex dial sweeps out every circle through the origin.

At k = i the wall sits at i. A Hermitian matrix has only real eigenvalues, so it can never reach that wall, and (I + iH)⁻¹(I − iH) is always defined and always unitary. That is the Cayley transform physicists use to turn energies into time steps, here read as the dial set to i.

A matrix on the dial draws your landscape biggest find

Put the 2×2 matrix U = [[0, −q], [1, k]] on the dial. Then (I + aU)(I + bU) = (1 − q·ab)I + (a + b + k·ab)U, and the ratio of the two parts is your landscape lens law (a + b + k·ab)/(1 − q·ab).

Everything on the landscape page reads off U. Its wall polynomial 1 + k·a + q·a² is det(I + aU). The walls sit at −1/eigenvalue, just as the plain dial's wall sits at −1/k. The three countries are the three kinds of 2×2 matrix: two real eigenvalues, one repeated, or a complex pair. The parabola k² = 4q is where the eigenvalues meet, since k² − 4q = (λ₁ − λ₂)². The "or" line k + q = −1 is where U has eigenvalue −1, one direction sitting exactly on mult-add's wall.

Rotation, shear, boost

The simplest matrices on the dial square to a number: U² = κ·I. Their law is (a + b)/(1 + κ·ab).

κ = −1: rotations and tangent addition, with no real wall. κ = 0: shears and plain addition, as in Galileo. κ = +1: Lorentz boosts and Einstein's velocity addition. det(I + aU) = (1 − a)(1 + a), so the walls are a = ±1, the speed of light. The light cone is the wall.

Known as the Cayley–Klein geometries (Yaglom, 1979). It is the same Euclid–Galileo–Einstein trio the early-laws page found, now coming out of one dial matrix.

A matrix inside the law standard

The weight can also sit between the factors: A ⊞_K B = A + B + AKB, which lifts through I + AK. Sylvester's identity det(I + AK) = det(I + KA) says the cube in one dimension equals the cube in another: a 3×5 A and a 5×3 K give the same volume. When K has rank one, every corner term vanishes and det(I + A·u·vᵀ) = 1 + vᵀAu. A rank-one weight turns the cube back into addition, the general form of the 2×2 coupling result above.
