P vs NP: what can't work, and where the circle operation touches it

The result in two sentences

The circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0) can't change P vs NP: on the shifted ruler it is ordinary multiplication, and Lean now proves that any circuit built from ∘₁ and shifted add turns into an ordinary circuit of exactly the same size, and back. Where it does say something real is the dial: folded over a problem's witnesses, k = −1 asks "is there one?" (NP), k = 0 asks "how many?" (#P), and k = −2 asks "odd or even?" (⊕P), which is a clean picture of known classes and a good landscape page, but not a route to a proof.

Your hunch is the mainstream one: almost everyone expects P ≠ NP. And you're right that there's no easy counterexample to look for. A counterexample would be an algorithm, and the map below is mostly a list of whole families of algorithms and whole families of proofs that are already known to fail.

1. Methods known not to prove P ≠ NP

Each barrier is a theorem. It doesn't say P = NP; it says a certain kind of argument can never settle the question.

Barrier What it kills Why, in one sentence
Relativization (Baker, Gill, Solovay 1975) Diagonalization and simulation: the arguments behind the time and space hierarchy theorems There is an oracle that makes P = NP and another that makes P ≠ NP, so any proof that would work unchanged with an oracle attached proves both, which is impossible.
Natural proofs (Razborov, Rudich 1994) Almost every combinatorial circuit lower bound we have: random restrictions (parity not in AC⁰), the Razborov–Smolensky polynomial method (AC⁰[p]), and their relatives A property of Boolean functions that is easy to check from the truth table and true of many functions, if it ruled out small circuits, would also tell pseudorandom functions apart from random ones, which would break cryptography we believe is secure.
Algebrization (Aaronson, Wigderson 2008) Arithmetization plus diagonalization: the techniques that got past relativization (IP = PSPACE, MIP = NEXP) Even when the oracle is replaced by its low-degree polynomial extension, there are worlds on both sides, so arithmetizing doesn't escape the relativization problem.
Monotone bounds don't transfer (Razborov 1985; Tardos 1988) Proving a lower bound for circuits without NOT gates and hoping it carries over Razborov proved CLIQUE needs huge monotone circuits, but he also proved perfect matching does, and matching is in P; Tardos found a problem in P whose monotone circuits are exponential, so monotone hardness says nothing about real hardness.
Occurrence obstructions in GCT (Bürgisser, Ikenmeyer, Panova, JAMS 2019) The simplest form of Mulmuley–Sohoni geometric complexity theory for permanent vs determinant The representations that were supposed to appear for the determinant and not the padded permanent always appear in both; the multiplicity version is still open.
Proof-complexity limits (Cook–Reckhow 1979; Razborov 1995) Hoping a weak logical system can prove the lower bound, and hoping a weak proof system can certify every tautology NP ≠ coNP is exactly "no proof system has short proofs of every tautology"; we have exponential lower bounds for resolution and bounded-depth Frege but none for full Frege, and Razborov showed weak bounded arithmetic can't prove strong circuit lower bounds if strong pseudorandom generators exist.
Algebraic natural proofs (Forbes–Shpilka–Volk; Grochow–Kumar–Saks–Saraf 2017) The algebraic version of the natural-proofs strategy for VP vs VNP If certain succinct hitting sets exist, the polynomial analogue of a natural proof can't separate VP from VNP; this one is conditional and less settled.

What has worked despite the barriers: Ryan Williams's 2011 proof that NEXP is not in ACC⁰ turned fast algorithms into lower bounds and avoids all three big barriers. It is far weaker than P ≠ NP but it shows the barriers can be stepped around.

2. Things that can't be the counterexample (routes to P = NP that are closed)

This is the counterexample-map spirit from the Fermat work: what can't work.

  • Any SAT solver whose runs are resolution proofs. Modern CDCL solvers are equivalent to resolution (Pipatsrisawat–Darwiche 2011), and Haken (1985) proved resolution needs exponential size on pigeonhole formulas. So those solvers must take exponential time there.
  • Any polynomial-size linear program for TSP. Fiorini, Massar, Pokutta, Tiwary and de Wolf (2012) proved the TSP polytope has exponential extension complexity. Rothvoß (2014) proved the same for perfect matching, even though matching is in P, so LPs aren't even the right measure of "easy." Many claimed proofs of P = NP were exactly these LPs.
  • Any pure dynamic program over min-plus. Jerrum and Snir (1982) proved monotone arithmetic circuits for the permanent need exponential size, and for homogeneous problems like assignment that transfers to min-plus (tropical) circuits. Yet assignment is solved in polynomial time by the Hungarian method, which uses subtraction and branching. So tropical lower bounds, like monotone ones, are real but don't reach general algorithms.
  • A universal search. Levin (1973) wrote down an algorithm that is polynomial for SAT search if P = NP at all. So if a counterexample exists, a version of it is already known; what nobody can do is prove its running time. The difficulty is proving, not finding.

3. What the quantum Fourier transform can and can't tell us

  • What it does: the QFT finds hidden periods. That gives exponential speedups for factoring and discrete log (Shor), and in general for the hidden subgroup problem over abelian groups.
  • Why that doesn't reach NP-complete problems: factoring isn't believed NP-complete. For black-box search, Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani (1997) proved quantum computers need about √N queries, so Grover's square-root speedup is the best possible; relative to a random oracle, NP is not inside BQP. Exponential speedups need hidden algebraic structure, and NP-complete problems in general don't offer it.
  • The nonabelian case fails too: for the symmetric group (the graph-isomorphism case), the standard Fourier sampling approach provably can't succeed (Moore, Russell, Schulman 2005; Hallgren et al.).
  • And it wouldn't settle P vs NP anyway: P vs NP is about classical computers. Even NP ⊆ BQP would not imply P = NP. Raz and Tal (2018) found an oracle where BQP is not inside the polynomial hierarchy, so quantum and classical classes sit sideways to each other.

4. Where the circle operation touches the question, judged on its own merits

4.1 The shifted ruler: no new power (proved)

With shifted add sadd(a, b) = a + b + 1 and the circle operation a ∘₁ b = a + b + ab, the map x ↦ 1 + x turns sadd into + and ∘₁ into ×, gate for gate. Lean (evalS_lift, eval_from_shift, size_mapC): every sadd/∘₁ circuit is a +/× circuit with the same wiring and size, and every +/× circuit is a sadd/∘₁ circuit with the same wiring and size. So circuit size, VP vs VNP, and every algebraic lower-bound question are identical in both worlds. Not a lead. This is the honest answer to "does anything change?": nothing does, and that's now a theorem rather than a hunch.

The circle operation a + b + k·ab is the same story for every k ≠ 0 (it's ∘₁ seen through x ↦ kx, CircleOp.circK_scale). At k = 0 it becomes plain addition. That's a contraction, not a tropical limit: the dial doesn't turn + into min.

4.2 The dial over witnesses: NP, #P, ⊕P and ModₚP at different settings (proved)

Fold the dial over a list of yes/no witnesses and you get the q-integer of the number of yes answers, with q = k + 1 (witness_fold):

Dial q The fold gives The class
k = −1 0 1 if any witness, else 0 NP ("is there one?"), np_point
k = 0 1 the number of witnesses #P ("how many?"), sharpP_point
k = −2 −1 1 if odd, 0 if even ⊕P ("odd or even?"), parityP_point
qᵖ = 1 p-th root of unity the count mod p ModₚP, modp_period
k = 1 2 2ᵐ − 1, the whole count #P again, qnum_two_strictMono

The picture is correct and tidy: NP is the one point on the dial that throws information away (np_point_forgets: one witness and five look the same at q = 0), and almost every other point keeps the count. It fits your theme of operations that keep information, with a twist: keeping information makes problems harder, not easier. Toda's theorem says counting (#P) is at least as strong as the whole polynomial hierarchy.

Judgment: the classes are all standard (this is the GapP and counting-class picture in new coordinates), and relativization already separates them in different ways in different oracle worlds, so the dial gives no new leverage on P vs NP. It is worth one landscape page for the project, because it puts NP, ⊕P, ModₚP and #P on one line as settings of the same operation, and the known hardness jumps (permanent easy mod 2, hard mod 3; Valiant 1979) become places where the landscape changes character.

4.3 Over the two-element field, the circle operation is the OR gate (proved)

Over GF(2), a + b + ab is a OR b, and x ↦ 1 + x is NOT, so the lift 1 + (a ∘₁ b) = (1 + a)(1 + b) is De Morgan's law (gf2_circ_is_or, gf2_lift_de_morgan). Over the integers, the k = −1 version a + b − ab is the standard arithmetized OR.

Judgment: this is where the circle operation lives most naturally in complexity theory, and it's the most instructive negative finding here. The arithmetized OR is exactly what drives IP = PSPACE and the Razborov–Smolensky lower bounds, and those two families are precisely what the algebrization and natural-proofs barriers cover. So any P ≠ NP argument built mainly from the circle operation's algebra on yes/no values sits inside known barrier territory. Not a lead for P vs NP, but a useful "what can't work" entry.

4.4 Easy because it factors (proved)

The fold 1 ∘₁ 1 ∘₁ … ∘₁ 1 over n inputs has 2ⁿ − 1 monomials but takes n − 1 gates (foldCirc_ones), because 1 + (fold) = (1 + x₁)⋯(1 + xₙ) factors. The hard counting problems (the permanent, #SAT) are hard because their sums don't factor: the constraints tie the variables together. The circle operation is the independent case. Not a lead; it explains why circle-operation circuits are always easy.

4.5 The min-plus end

The dynamic-programming algebra (min, +) is a semiring (minplus_distrib), and the same polynomial changes difficulty with the semiring: the permanent is perfect-matching existence over Booleans (P), the assignment problem over min-plus (P, but tropical circuits need exponential size), the determinant over GF(2) (P), and #P-complete over the integers. Tropical circuit complexity is an active area where real lower bounds get proved (Jukna's work). Judgment: a place where project-style algebra could prove real theorems, since lower bounds there are provable. It won't reach P vs NP, because, like monotone bounds, they don't transfer to general algorithms.

5. Leads worth following

  • For P vs NP itself: none from the circle operation. The shifted ruler is provably the same computation, and the dial's natural home (arithmetized OR, counting) is covered by known barriers. Saying so plainly is the useful result.
  • For the project: one, the dial landscape of counting classes (4.2), drawn as a page: NP at q = 0, ⊕P at q = −1, ModₚP at roots of unity, #P everywhere else, with the permanent's known jumps marked. It's a real organising picture of known math, not a new prediction.

  • size_mapC, evalS_lift, eval_from_shift: circuits transport across the shifted ruler at equal size
