Every structure mult-add builds is a familiar one, moved one step to the left

Slide every number up by 1 and mult-add becomes plain multiplication. So mult-add makes a group, rings, a module and a torus, each one an exact copy of a structure algebra already knows. The new things are the pictures and a few clean tables, not new structures.

Mult-add is multiplication in disguise, so every group, ring and quotient it makes is a known one: ring theorists call it the circle operation, and its units are the quasi-regular elements of the Jacobson radical. No lead here is as promising as the solitons. The most useful finds are a sharp test for when a clock of numbers wraps into a group, and that a law's country can change from prime to prime.

The weighted family is one group, measured in different units

For every weight k other than 0, multiplying by k turns a ⊕k b = a + b + k·ab into plain mult-add: k(a ⊕k b) = (ka) ⊕ (kb). The wall moves from −1 to −1/k, nothing else changes. So all nonzero weights give the same group, and k = 0 (plain addition) is the only different one: addition has no element that undoes itself, while mult-add has −2, since (−2) ⊕ (−2) = 0.

On the whole numbers the units are tiny. Only 0 and −2 can be undone under ⊕, because only ±1 can be undone under ×. Weights ±1 and ±2 are the only ones with a second unit, and weight −2 on {0, 1} is exactly exclusive or, the parity wall from earlier pages. proved

Unique factoring carries over too: the positive whole numbers that can't be split as a ⊕ b are exactly p − 1 for primes p: 1, 2, 4, 6, 10, 12, 16. checked to 60

Clock arithmetic: when does mult-add wrap into a group?

On a clock of n numbers, plain mult-add is never a group, because the wall n − 1 swallows everything. Its units are the clock's ordinary units shifted down by one, φ(n) of them. On a 12-clock they are 0, 4, 6, 10. proved

The weighted clock (ℤ/n, ⊕k) is a group exactly when every prime of n divides k, because then k is nilpotent and 1 + ka can always be undone (a geometric series that stops). proved in general for nilpotent k, exact for n = 8, 9, 12 checked for every n up to 40

Which group it is follows one rule, the p-adic logarithm: odd pm with k = p gives the plain cycle; powers of 2 need k divisible by 4 to be a cycle, and with k = 2 they split, like the Klein four-group (ℤ/4, ⊕₂), which is not (ℤ/4, +). The clock (ℤ/n, ⊕k) is exactly the units ≡ 1 mod k in the clock of size kn. Klein and cyclic cases proved identification checked to n = 30

Rings: the fused pair is one, rung 3 is a module, rung 4 is nothing

Rungs 1 and 2 form a ring. Shifted addition a ⊞ b = a + b + 1 and mult-add obey every ring law, with zero −1 and one 0, and the shift carries them to (+, ×) exactly. Mult-add distributes over shifted addition but not over plain addition. proved The axiom thread's MA-ring takes the other pairing (plain + with ⊕) and recovers × from it; the two meet at the same ring.

Rung 3 is a module action, not a product. The fused exponent spreads over mult-add, (a ⊕ b)⊛s = (a⊛s) ⊕ (b⊛s), and its exponents combine by the fused ring: a⊛(s ⊞ t) = (a⊛s) ⊕ (a⊛t), (a⊛s)⊛t = a⊛(s ⊕ t). That makes (ℝ above −1, ⊕) a vector space whose scalars are the fused ring, the old fact that positive numbers under × are a vector space with powers as scalars. proved for whole-number scalars checked for fractions

Rung 4 and up carry no ring structure. The shift keeps every law, so fused tetration fails exactly where tetration fails: not commutative, not associative, and it spreads over neither mult-add nor the fused exponent. (1, 1, 1 happens to agree, 255 both ways; 1, 2, 1 does not.) failures over mult-add proved in the library over the fused exponent checked

If you want a ring at every rung, there is a known choice. Bennett (1915) built rungs that stay commutative: c(a, b) = (1 + a)ln(1+b) − 1. It is commutative, associative, spreads over mult-add, and its one is e − 1. Each such rung is the previous pair seen through ln(1 + x). It is a different ladder from your hyperoperation ladder, and it is a copy of (+, ×) at every step. checked to 10⁻¹² Bennett 1915

Polynomials and Pascal

Mult-add powers are the cube: a⊕n = (1 + a)n − 1 = Σ C(n, j) aj, one layer of Pascal per row of corners. Going back uses Pascal with alternating signs. The two matrices are the shift x ↦ x + 1 and its inverse, and the shift is e raised to differentiation (Taylor's theorem as a matrix).

The power maps are the endomorphisms of mult-add: (a ⊕ b)⊕n = a⊕n ⊕ b⊕n and (a⊕m)⊕n = a⊕mn. So the whole numbers act on mult-add as a ring, and over the p-adic numbers the binomial coefficients C(c, j) for any p-adic c extend it. proved Mahler, formal groups

On power series with no constant term there is no wall at all, so mult-add is a group there: these are the big Witt vectors. checked Witt 1937

The Tate torus is a quotient group of mult-add

Declare a ∼ b when (1 + a) and (1 + b) differ by a power of q. That relation is a congruence for mult-add: glued inputs give glued outputs. So mult-add passes to the quotient, which is a commutative monoid, and a group off the wall on a field. The class of q − 1 is the identity. proved, with Lean's quotient type

Over the complex numbers with |q| < 1 the quotient is ℂ*/qℤ, an elliptic curve, the elliptic page's torus. Over the reals with 0 < q < 1 it is a circle and a second circle (two components); over a prime field it is a finite cyclic group. Tate 1959

The landscape's countries are the three kinds of one-dimensional group, and they change with the prime

Your guess holds. Two walls is the split torus (a copy of × with p − 1 points mod p). Turning is the non-split torus (rotations, p + 1 points). The border is the additive group (p points). These are the only three connected one-dimensional algebraic groups. counted for 6 laws, 9 primes classical

The country is decided by the discriminant k² − 4q, and mod p by whether it is a square. Slope addition (k = 0, q = 1) has discriminant −4, so it grows two walls exactly at primes p ≡ 1 mod 4, the primes that are sums of two squares (5 = 1 + 4, 13 = 4 + 9). It turns at 3, 7, 11. Mult-add always has two walls. walls mod 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 proved

Known or new

Known. The circle operation a + b + ab and quasi-regular elements (Perlis 1942, Jacobson 1945); radical rings; the multiplicative formal group and its endomorphisms; principal units of ℤ/n and the p-adic logarithm; Bennett's commutative hyperoperations (1915); the Tate curve (1959); one-dimensional tori and the additive group; Witt vectors. Every structure on this page is one of these, seen through the shift.

Possibly fresh, as presentation only. One table that says, for each weight k and clock n, whether mult-add wraps into a group and which group; and the reading of a landscape point's country as a Legendre symbol, which ties the turning country to Fermat's two-square primes. Neither is a new theorem.

Inferred, not proved here. The general identification of (ℤ/n, ⊕k) with principal units and its cyclic or split form (checked, follows from the p-adic logarithm); the country counts for all primes (checked, classical); Bennett's rung (real logs, outside core Lean).
