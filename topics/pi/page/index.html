Pi is the length of the trip from 0 to −2 around the circle operation's wall

Through the turning law's lens, every real slope sits on one circle of radius 1 drawn around the wall at −1. That circle crosses the number line at just two points: 0, where you start, and −2, the parity point. Pi is how far the turning flow runs to get from one to the other. The circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0) alone then gives you the digits. The hunt for a new transcendental found one number nobody seems to have named, B½(1,1) = 2.40717…, but whether it is transcendental can't be proved with today's math.

Where pi lives: half of the circle operation's own period

The turning law is slope addition, x ⊕ y = (x + y)/(1 − xy), at the landscape point (0, 1). Write each slope as the Gaussian number 1 + i·x. Then slope addition is just multiplication: (1 + ix)(1 + iy) = (1 − xy)(1 + i·(x ⊕ y)). The lens 1 + a = (1 + ix)/(1 − ix) makes this exact, so a(x ⊕ y) = a(x) ⊞ a(y). The turning law is the circle operation with complex walls.

The lens puts every real slope on the circle |1 + a| = 1, centred on the wall −1. The circle operation keeps you on that circle, because lengths multiply. Slope 0 lands on a = 0, slope 1 on a = −1 + i, and slope ∞ on a = −2. On the real line the circle is exactly the two points 0 and −2.

So the turning flow starts at 0, passes through infinity exactly when the circle operation reaches −2, and comes back to 0. The distance to −2 in the law's own units is pi; the full loop is 2π. In the circle operation's own terms, its logarithm log(1 + a) gains 2πi each time you go once round the wall.

Every turning law has period 2π ÷ √(4q − p²)

The law (a + b + p·ab)/(1 − q·ab) grows by y′ = 1 + p·y + q·y². Under the parabola (4q > p²) the flow blows up and comes round, and the time it takes is 2π/√(4q − p²). Pi is the turning country's one constant; the distance from the parabola only sets the scale. On the parabola the period runs off to infinity. Above it, in two-wall country, the same formula gives an imaginary period, which is a logarithm. Pi and log are one quantity seen from either side of the parabola.

Three ways to get the digits using the circle operation only

1. Count tiny steps

Combine i/n with itself by the circle operation until you pass −2. Pure counting, no logarithms, but slow: you get about one digit for each factor of 10 in n.

nSteps to pass −2Steps ÷ n

n ≤ 1,000 counted exactly with Gaussian integers; n = 10, 100 and 1,000 are also checked by Lean's kernel.

2. Halve the trip (Archimedes)

Find the step a with 3 copies landing on −2, then halve it again and again (a half-step b solves b ⊞ b = a). The straight lengths of all the steps sit below pi; the same steps measured in slope units sit above it. 48 steps is exactly Archimedes' 96-sided polygon.

Steps 0 → −2BelowAbove

3. Land on the eighth turn, then take the way back

Four steps of i/5 and one step back of i/239 land exactly on the 45° ray, an eighth of the full turn: (5 + i)⁴(239 − i) = 114,244·(1 + i). Then the circle operation's logarithm, L(a) = a − a²/2 + a³/3 − …, turns the steps back into addition, and its imaginary part at a = ix is x − x³/3 + x⁵/5 − … So pi = 4·(4·L(i/5) − L(i/239)) in imaginary parts. This is Machin's formula from 1706, written in the circle operation.

A search over mixes of two step sizes finds exactly the four that land on the eighth turn, the same four Størmer proved are the only ones in 1899: Machin, Euler (i/2 and i/3), Hermann and Hutton.

The Euler limit is a fourth view of the same thing: n copies of iπ/n combined by the circle operation head for −2 (−2.0000049 at n = 1,000,000), because (1 + iπ/n)ⁿ → e^{iπ} = −1. It needs pi to state, so it is a check rather than a route to the digits.











Hunting another transcendental: one unnamed number, nothing new proved

Each was also tested for being a root of an integer polynomial up to degree 12. The search caught every relation that should exist, which shows it works.

Proving it transcendental is out of reach: nobody can even prove e + π is irrational, and this number has no exponential form for Lindemann–Weierstrass, Gelfond–Schneider or Baker to grip. open

Feigenbaum sits on the road from the circle operation to the turning law


Doubling in the circle operation is u ↦ u² on the lift (the map z² + c at c = 0). Doubling in the turning law, read on the circle as z = u + 1/u, is z ↦ z² − 2 (c = −2). These are the only two real quadratic maps that can be solved exactly. Everything between them is the period-doubling cascade, whose rate is Feigenbaum's δ ≈ 4.6692016. Your elliptic curves supply the third solvable kind (Lattès maps). Feigenbaum's constants are believed transcendental but not even proved irrational. known







Where z² + c settles, for c from 0 (circle doubling, left) to −2 (turning doubling, right). Superstable points computed here give δ = 4.669201596 at period 2¹⁴.

Known or new






Known. Every route to pi here is a re-derivation: Archimedes' polygons (about 250 BC), Gregory and Leibniz's series (1670s), Machin (1706), Euler's formula, the Cayley transform, Størmer's list (1899). That the circle operation's logarithm has period 2πi is the textbook fact about the multiplicative group. Lindemann and Gelfond–Schneider settle the provable cases. The half-iterate value E½(1) = 1.27102741388995… is published (Finch, 2025, after Morris and Szekeres, 1961).






New here. The single picture: the circle round the wall through 0 and −2, with pi as the trip between them; pi and log as one period on either side of the landscape parabola; the Lean checks; and the number B½(1,1) = 2.40717…, which I could not find anywhere. No lead reaches the soliton level: nothing here is a new theorem, and the new number is new as a name, not as a proof.
