Two boosts in different directions leave a twist, and the twist is an area

In one direction, relativity's velocity addition is the circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0) in disguise and order never matters. Push in two directions and the result is a boost plus a small rotation. In the circle-operation picture that rotation comes from one place: the corner ū·v of the fused law turns complex, and its cross part sets the angle. The angle is exactly d times the area of the velocity triangle, where d is the landscape's own number, now read as the curvature of the space of velocities.

Twist ω

Where the twist comes from

The relativity page wrote a boost as the matrix [[1, u], [u, 1]] and found B(u)·B(v) = (1 + uv)·B(u⊕v). In the plane a speed is a complex number, and the boost of the dial is [[1, u], [−d·ū, 1]]. Multiply two:

B(u)·B(v) = [[1 − d·u·v̄, u + v], [−d·(ū + v̄), 1 − d·ū·v]]

The two diagonal entries were equal in one dimension. Now they are conjugates, X + iY and X − iY, because the corner splits: ū·v = (u·v) + i(u × v). The dot part is the old corner. The cross part is new, and it is twice the area of the triangle 0, u, v.

twist eiω = (X + iY)/(X − iY),  tan(ω/2) = q(u × v) / (1 − q(u·v))

It is 1 exactly when d·(u × v) = 0: boosts along one line, or the Galilean dial d = 0. The same twist is all that order changes: u⊕v = twist · (v⊕u), with u⊕v = (u + v)/(1 − d·ū·v).

Why it is an area

Carry a gyroscope around the triangle rest → u → u⊕v → rest, along straight paths in velocity space. It comes back turned by ω. On a curved surface that turning is the curvature times the enclosed area (Gauss–Bonnet), and in the disk picture the curvature is exactly d. So:

ω = d × (area of the velocity triangle)

Relativity has d = −1/c², so velocity space is a saddle and the gyroscope turns backwards. d = 0 is flat: Galilean boosts never twist. d = +1 is a sphere: these "boosts" are rotations, and the turn is the familiar spherical excess. One formula covers all three; only the sign and size of d change.

For slow speeds the triangle is nearly flat, so ω ≈ −(u × v)/(2c²) in ordinary velocities: tiny, which is why everyday boosts look like they commute.

Thomas precession: the area of a circle of velocities

Move in a circle at steady speed and your velocity traces a circle in velocity space. Each step is a small boost in a new direction, so a gyroscope you carry keeps twisting. After one lap the total is the area inside that velocity circle:

per lap: 2π(γ − 1) = 2π(cosh φ − 1)

Checked by composing 20,000 small 4×4 Lorentz boosts around the lap: at 0.3c, 0.6c and 0.9c the gyroscope turns by exactly 2π(γ − 1). At 0.6c it is a quarter turn per lap.

Gravity Probe B: two twists, one measurement

A gyroscope orbiting Earth picks up two twists each orbit, and both are curvature times area. The velocity twist is the Thomas twist above: relative to observers standing still in Earth's gravity (who are pushed upward), the gyroscope's velocity goes round a circle, and velocity space has curvature d = −1/c². The space twist comes from space itself being curved: carry an arrow round the orbit in the curved space slice and it comes back turned by the curvature it encloses.

Exactly, the two twists combine by multiplying their factors: 1 − p = (1 − pspace)(1 − pvelocity), with pspace = 1 − √(1 − 2M/r) and pvelocity = 1 − 1/γ. In the circle operation that is −p = (−pspace) ∘₁ (−pvelocity), and it reproduces the full general-relativity answer 1 − √(1 − 3M/r) per orbit, checked against a direct numerical parallel transport in the Schwarzschild geometry. The velocity factor matches Thomas's twist to first order, which is all Gravity Probe B can see; the exact factor carries 1/γ rather than γ, so that part of the reading holds only to first order.

Frame dragging fits the same picture in another form. Earth's spin gives space a "gravitomagnetic" twist field, and the gyroscope's turn rate is that field's circulation per unit area (Stokes). Averaged over the polar orbit it gives about 41 mas/yr against the measured 37.2 ± 7.2 (the mission's refined figure is 39.2).

The double pulsar: three twists

In a binary pulsar the gyroscope is the pulsar itself, and its partner is not a fixed Earth but a star of similar mass swinging round the same centre. The same two twists appear, now weighted by the companion's mass m₂: the velocity twist ½·m₂/M and the space twist m₂/M. A third joins them, the companion's drag 2·m₁/M: the companion moves, so its gravitomagnetic field circulates round the pulsar, the frame-dragging twist again. Added up, the three give m₂(4m₁ + 3m₂)/(2M), which is exactly the standard spin-orbit coefficient, and with a light gyroscope (m₁ → 0) they fall back to the Gravity Probe B law.

And although its field is about 7,000 times Gravity Probe B's (M/r ≈ 4 × 10⁻⁶), the ∘₁ corner that separates the exact fusion from a plain sum is still about a millionth of the effect, so this test cannot see the exact fusion either. The same bookkeeping gives 1.21°/yr for the Hulse–Taylor pulsar, whose beam is seen to change shape at that slow rate.

Half a kick

The disk uses half-velocities: t = tanh(φ/2), the speed that, added to itself, gives the real speed (t ⊕ t = 2t/(1 + t²) = β). On the circle-operation ruler of the relativity page that is the circle-operation square root: d(t ⊕ t) = d(t) ∘₁ d(t). Half-velocities are the coordinates in which the corner splits cleanly, so the half kick is the natural unit for the twist.

Is this a lead worth following?

Yes, and the Gravity Probe B test made it stronger. Twist = curvature × area, used twice (once in velocity space, once in space), gives 6,604 mas/yr against the measured 6,601.8 ± 18.3, and neither twist alone comes close. The exact answer even comes out as a circle operation of the two twist fractions. That is a real physical number reproduced by the picture, not only a restatement.

What it is not: new physics. The split into a one-third velocity part and a two-thirds space part is textbook (de Sitter 1916, Schiff 1960). What is ours is the reading: both parts are the same twist law, and they fuse by ∘₁. The double pulsar then fits too: three twists give 5.07°/yr against the measured 4.77 ± 0.66, and the data needs the companion's drag twist.

Where it stops: neither test can see the exact ∘₁ fusion, because the corner is second order and even the double pulsar's field leaves it about a millionth of the effect. Gyroscope data in a much stronger field does not exist yet, so for now the fusion is a correct exact identity, checked against theory, but not yet tested by measurement.
