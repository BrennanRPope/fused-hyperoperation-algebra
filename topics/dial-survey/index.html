The five dials come down to two, plus a picture and a setting

Of the five dials, only two are independent numbers: the weight k and the one-dial-per-element t. The prime dial is a slice of the per-element dial, the twist is k seen in two dimensions, and choice is a setting, not a number. The one new thing in this pass is what happens when k acts on per-element dials: k becomes "how many copies of an element are allowed", and on the primes that gives one family running from ζ(s)/ζ(2s) to ζ(s), with necklace counts as exponents.

1. The k dial (q = k + 1)

What it is. a ✳ₖ b = a + b + k·ab, with 1 + k(a ✳ₖ b) = (1 + ka)(1 + kb). In units λ times bigger, weight k reads as k·λ, so on bare numbers only the sign of k survives: k > 0 is the circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0), k = 0 is addition, k < 0 is "union of chances".

Connects to. Already found across the project: q-counting and knots (q = k + 1), Potts colours, Sugeno's λ-measures, fermion/Poisson/boson point processes (k = −1, 0, +1). It is also Tsallis's q-algebra from statistical physics, with k = 1 − qTsallis.

What following it gives. On its own, nothing more: a one-parameter family whose only invariant is a sign is fully classified. Its size starts to matter only once elements carry their own dials, because then the dial values fix the units (section "k on elements").

Not a lead by itself. It's the axis the other dials are measured against. The separate search for a potential on k is the place where its size could get a physical meaning.

2. The twist

What it is. Composing two boosts in different directions leaves a turn eiω with tan(ω/2) = q(u×v)/(1 − q·u·v). That's q times the signed area of the velocity triangle: Gauss–Bonnet with curvature q.

Connects to. Thomas precession (1926), Wigner rotation (1939). Already matched data on the twisted-boosts page: Gravity Probe B's 6.6 arcsec/yr and the double pulsar's spin precession.

What following it gives. The project's firmest geometric underpinning: q is curvature, and the twist is how curvature shows up. But the twist has no setting of its own. It vanishes exactly when q·(u×v) = 0, so it's fixed by q and the directions. And the exact fusion inside it (−p = (−pspace) ✳ (−pvelocity)) is an identity of general relativity, so no measurement can split the two.

Not a separate dial, and no new prediction. Worth keeping as the geometric anchor your rule asks for, not as a path to follow further.

3. One dial per element

What it is. Each element x gets tx; a subset weighs the product of its members' dials, and the total is ∏(1 + tx). Exactly: t is odds, p = t/(1 + t). "In A or in B" for independent random subsets has odds t ✳ s, and complement turns odds t into 1/t. A uniform dial t = 2α − 1 gives counts 2αn, and dials combine by the circle operation exactly when exponents add, so this dial and the fractional power set's exponent are one dial read through the circle-operation ruler.

Connects to. Bernoulli product measures; a lattice gas with fugacity t on each site (partition function ∏(1 + t)); Lee–Yang zeros.

What following it gives. A half power set that exists in weighted form (every dial √2 − 1), where the set-theoretic half is blocked by choice. Weights aren't sizes, though, so it doesn't settle the ZF question. The merging and complement rules are worked out on Power set dials.

A lead, but only paired with k. On its own it's the measure-theory shadow of the fractional power set. With k acting on it, it produces new structure (below).

4. The prime dial

What it is. The per-element dial on the set of primes with tp = p−s. That's a one-parameter slice of dial 3, not a new dial. ∏(1 + p−s) = ζ(s)/ζ(2s), the squarefree numbers.

Connects to. The fermionic primon gas (Julia 1990, Spector 1990): s is inverse temperature, energies are log p, and s = 1 is the Hagedorn point where it boils.

What following it gives. Alone, known number theory. With k it becomes the two-dial family below, which is the clearest place two dials interact.

Same dial as 3, on primes. Its value is as the test bed for k on elements.

5. The choice dial

What it is. How much of the axiom of choice holds. The earlier pass found a short ladder, by which rung goes flat on infinite sizes: successor flat is weak, addition flat is stronger, multiplication flat is full choice (Tarski 1924), and the exponent is never flat.

Connects to. The Howard–Rubin map of choice principles. That map branches: the Boolean prime ideal theorem and dependent choice are independent of each other, so choice grades form a partial order, not a number line.

What following it gives. The setting where a true fractional power set might exist: without choice, sizes can fall forever, so the halving obstruction goes away. q only switches between rungs there, and the twist does nothing, because size arithmetic commutes.

Not a dial, but the path through it is a lead. The open question is the fractional power set in ZF; the next step stays a permutation model whose orbit counts grow like ec√n.

k acting on per-element dials

Give one element weight k and dial z. It contributes (1 − kz)−1/k, whose ze coefficient is (1)(1 + k)(1 + 2k)…(1 + (e−1)k)/e!. That reads as how many copies of the element are allowed:

The whole family is one formula: Zk(s) = ∏p(1 − k·p−s)−1/k = ∏N ζ(Ns)M(k,N)/k, where M(k,N) counts aperiodic necklaces of N beads in k colours: colourings of a regular N-gon up to rotation, a geometric count. Only k = ±1 use finitely many ζ factors. Every other k needs ζ(Ns) for infinitely many N, whose singularities pile up on Re s = 0, the way the prime zeta function's do (Landau–Walfisz 1920). That wall holds for every k except ±1, and it turns out to be known (see the follow-up below).

How common the k-weighted whole numbers are: their count up to x is about Ck·x, with Ck = ∏p(1 − 1/p)(1 − k/p)−1/k. Exact end points: C−1 = 6/π², C0 = eM−γ ≈ 0.7293 (Meissel–Mertens minus Euler's constant), C1 = 1. Direct counts to 300,000 match at k = ±½. Past k = 2 the factor for the prime 2 hits its wall first, and the boiling point moves from s = 1 to s = log₂k.

A small lead, now followed up. It's the one place where two dials make a structure neither has alone, and the exponents have a geometric reading (rotations of a polygon). The pieces are known (primon gases, the necklace identity of Metropolis and Rota, Selberg–Delange densities), so expect known results. The follow-up below checked the two open bits, the wall at Re s = 0 and the Lee–Yang match. Both close.

Follow-up: the wall, and Lee–Yang

Every k except ±1 hits a wall at Re s = 0, but that's already known: it's a special case of Dahlquist's 1952 theorem on Euler products. The "at most m copies" points k = −1/m don't line up with the Lee–Yang points; the two families meet only at k = −1. Both leads close.

Why ±1 are the only safe settings

Zk is a product of ζ(Ns) over all N, and each ζ zero ρ makes a singularity at ρ/N. Its strength is M(k,N)/k, a necklace count: colourings of a regular N-gon up to rotation. A whole number gives a harmless pole or zero; anything else gives a branch point. At k = +1 every count from N = 2 on is zero, and at k = −1 every count from N = 3 on is zero, so only finitely many ζ factors are left. At any other k the counts stay alive for all N.

Why the wall is solid

Column N of singular points sits at real part 1/(2N). As N grows the columns close in on Re s = 0 and fill it with no gaps, so there's no way to continue Zk past that line.

Lee–Yang

The Lee–Yang points 1 + 2cos(2π/n) start at −1, 0, 1 and climb to 3, all at or above 0 from n = 3 on. The copies points −1, −1/2, −1/3, … sit between −1 and 0. So they share only k = −1, and k = 0 as a limit. They can't match elsewhere: every Lee–Yang point is an algebraic integer, and −1/m isn't one for m ≥ 2.

Both leads close. The wall is known and the Lee–Yang match is only at the integer points, so there's no line for the catalog. What's worth keeping is the picture: the strength of each singularity is a polygon count, and k = ±1 are exactly the settings where those counts switch off.

Same dial in disguise

  • q and k: q = k + 1, the same dial shifted. Also the Potts colour count and the knot gluing number.
  • Twist and k: the twist is q × area, fixed by k and the directions.
  • Prime dial and per-element dial: the slice tp = p−s on the primes.
  • Per-element dial and fractional exponent: t = 2α − 1; dials combine by the circle operation when exponents add.
  • k on elements and point processes: "copies allowed" is the fermion/Poisson/boson dial already found in point processes.
  • Choice: independent of all of them, and not numeric.
