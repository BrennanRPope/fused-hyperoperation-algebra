Twisting the oxygen clock

Reversing the clock gives a real, different reaction: respiration's oxygen-eating enzyme runs the same four-step square the other way. Undoing the clock in time is forbidden, and every other twist lands on chemistry that already exists.

Lead check: nothing here is as promising as the soliton cube. The one twist nobody has made work is a clock that runs both ways at no cost, and the reason is a geometric one: the four quarter turns can't be made equal.

Fused Hyperoperation Algebra, rung 1: x ∘₁ y = x + y + xy. The clock's flash step is the point 1 + x = λ in the complex plane, and n flashes are the n-fold circle operation ∘₁ (called mult-add before 1.0), λⁿ. Numbers from twists.py beside this page.

Reversed, the clock becomes respiration's oxygen enzyme

Photosystem II pulls four electrons out of two waters, one per flash, and lets O₂ go on the fourth step. Cytochrome c oxidase, the last enzyme of respiration, does the exact reverse: it binds O₂ and pushes four electrons into it one at a time, from cytochrome c, until two waters leave. Its catalytic states are named R, A, P, F, O, E, and four of the links between them are single electron steps. So the same square exists in nature, run the other way, in nearly every cell of yours.

The numbers. Splitting water needs electrons lifted from +0.818 V to NADPH at −0.324 V, 1.14 V each, paid for with two red photons (1.82 eV each). The reverse enzyme drops electrons from cytochrome c at +0.254 V to oxygen, 0.564 V each, which releases 218 kJ per O₂. It uses that to pump four protons across the membrane.

A surprise from the picture. Reversing the clock mirrors λ across the real axis, and I checked that the oxygen-per-flash pattern comes out identical, to every digit. The pattern can't tell which way the clock turns. Only the chemistry at the corners can: which state lets O₂ go, and which one takes it in.

Flipping the sign: one version is respiration, the other is forbidden

"Switch the sign" splits into two different moves.

Flip the energy's sign. Run the whole reaction downhill: sugar and oxygen back to carbon dioxide and water, releasing the 2870 kJ per glucose that light stored. That is respiration, or a fire. Real, and the reverse enzyme above is its last step.

Flip the step's sign. In the circle operation the inverse of x is −x / (1 + x), the value that undoes it: (1 + x)(1 + y) = 1. Applied to the clock, that would un-flash it, running the probabilities backwards in time. The point moves from λ, inside the circle, to 1/λ, outside it at distance 1.124. Starting from a clock that is surely in S1, one step back already needs a population of −0.108, and after five steps one state is at −0.916. No real mixture has negative amounts.

This is the second law in the picture. A clock made of chances can only shrink toward the steady state, so its points stay inside the unit circle. The inverse sends them outside, where only growth lives. The reverse chemistry is fine; reversing the forgetting is not.

Mirroring the handedness is allowed but changes nothing you can weigh

The sugar

Life makes only D-glucose. Its mirror twin, L-glucose, has exactly the same energy, so a mirror plant would store the same 2870 kJ per sugar from the same light. The difference predicted from the weak force has never been measured in any molecule. One soil bacterium, Paracoccus 43P, was found in 2012 to eat L-glucose with its own pathway, so nature does touch the mirror sugar, but nothing makes it from light.

A whole mirror plant would need every protein and nucleic acid mirrored too. That is "mirror life", and in December 2024 a large group of scientists warned in Science that mirror bacteria could evade immune defences and recommended not building them. It's chemically possible and deliberately left alone.

The electron chain

Proteins can't have true mirror symmetry, but reaction centres come close: two near-twin branches related by a half turn. In purple bacteria electrons go almost only down one branch. Mutants built since the 1990s can push some down the twin branch, more slowly. So the mirrored chain is real and works, just worse.

The light

Because chlorophyll's packing is chiral, leaves reflect light that is very slightly circular: about 7 parts in 10,000 in the field, near 680 nm, with a negative band just below and a positive band just above. Patty and colleagues use this as a sign of life from kilometres away. A mirror plant would show the same curve upside down.

Fewer sides gives sulfide photosynthesis, one side gives the retinal pumps

Change the number of corners and the same wound Pascal row describes other real cycles. With the same miss chance (0.084) each one is the point λ = 0.084 + 0.916·e^(2πi/n).

  • Two sides, sulfide: green sulfur bacteria take two electrons from H₂S and drop sulfur, with no oxygen and no four-step clock. Their donor sits at −0.27 V, so one photosystem is enough: 4 photons per CO₂ by electron count, against 8 for plants. The cube loses a dimension twice, square to line and two photosystems to one.
  • One side, light pumps: bacteriorhodopsin and the ocean's proteorhodopsin close their whole loop on every photon and pump one proton. With λ = 1 there is no flash pattern at all. A 568 nm photon (2.18 eV) moves one proton across about 0.2 V, roughly 9% stored.
  • Eight sides, nitrogen: nitrogenase counts eight electrons per N₂ (Lowe–Thorneley states E0 to E8), each delivered with two ATP. It is driven by chemistry, not flashes, so this octagon is a picture, not a flash experiment.

The sides change how fast the pattern fades: the two-sided clock's rhythm halves every 3.8 steps, the square's every 8.3, the octagon's every 30.

Running the chain uphill is real: chemosynthesis does it with a proton gradient

Photosynthesis lifts electrons with light. Respiration lets them fall. A third case lifts them with neither: bacteria that live on nitrite take electrons at +0.433 V and push them up to NADH at −0.320 V, 0.753 V uphill, 145 kJ per NADH. They pay by letting protons flow back in, so the respiratory chain runs backwards. At a 0.15 to 0.20 V gradient that costs roughly 4 to 5 protons per electron.

Purple bacteria do the same trick after their photosystem, because it only reaches quinone. So "run it the other way" isn't one reaction; it's a family that cells use whenever their electron source sits too high on the ladder.

Turning the square and flipping the cube both match lab results

Rotate the start. Turning the square by two corners means starting two steps behind. Hydroxylamine does exactly this: it reduces the cluster to two states below S0 in the dark, and the first oxygen burst moves from flash 3 to flash 5 (Messinger and colleagues, 1991). The model with two extra states reproduces flash 5.

Swap hit and miss. The cube's opposite-corner move turns a clock that almost always advances into one that almost never does: λ moves to 0.86 + 0.084i, a turn of only 5.6° per flash, a rhythm of 64 flashes that fades long before it shows. That is a frozen or blocked photosystem II: cold and some inhibitors raise the miss chance. Real, and dull.

The open twist: a clock that runs both ways at no cost

Put the two clocks together and ask for one catalyst that splits water just above 1.23 V and makes it again just below, wasting nothing. Thermodynamics allows it. Hydrogenase enzymes already do this for hydrogen, the two-sided clock, running both directions with almost no loss.

For oxygen nobody has it, and the picture says why. Each of the four steps should cost 1.23 eV, a perfect quarter turn. But on ordinary catalyst surfaces the two middle intermediates, OH and OOH, both hang on by one oxygen bond, so their energies move together, 3.2 eV apart. The two middle steps then need at least 1.6 eV each: turns of 117° instead of 90°, and a wasted 0.37 V at best. The square can't be made regular. Two steps have one intermediate and one dial; four steps have three intermediates but, on these surfaces, only two free dials.

Honest status: the 0.37 V floor and its cause are known (Rossmeisl, Nørskov and colleagues). The equal-steps idea itself is also published: chemists call it the electrochemical-step symmetry index (Govindarajan and colleagues, 2018), and the both-ways version is the bifunctional index, whose best value on these surfaces is 0.74 V (Busch and colleagues, 2016). It's plausible in principle and blocked by kinetics, not forbidden. The test below checks it against catalyst data.

The four steps of water oxidation as arcs of a full turn (4.92 eV). Left, the ideal clock. Right, the best clock allowed by the 3.2 eV link.

Tested on catalysts: the quarter turns restate the standard picture and don't beat it

The largest arc is exactly the overpotential, by arithmetic: an arc is 90° × (step energy ÷ 1.23 eV), so the biggest arc's excess over 90° is the limiting step's excess over 1.23 V. On four real catalysts with published energies it reproduces every published overpotential and symmetry index to the digit. That is the standard limiting-step method, drawn as a clock.

Do the other arcs add anything? I drew 19,321 catalysts around the published 3.2 ± 0.2 eV link between OH and OOH and asked how well each measure predicts the overpotential. The usual single number, the O minus OH energy, did best overall. The average of the too-big arcs (the published symmetry index) did slightly better among the good catalysts only, by about 0.02 V, which is far below what calculations can resolve.

Where the shape does matter. For a catalyst that has to work both ways, splitting and remaking water, the total loss is the biggest arc minus the smallest. The usual single number explains less than a third of that; the full clock gives it exactly, because it also needs the OH energy, a second dial. The best possible on the 3.2 eV line came out at 0.74 V, the same value published for the best two-way catalyst.

Against experiment. The big tables pairing calculated energies with measured overpotentials sit in supplementary files on a publisher site this browser can't reach, so I couldn't run that comparison myself. Calculated adsorption energies carry roughly 0.2 to 0.5 eV of error, and at that size two catalysts 0.1 V apart get ranked the wrong way round a third to almost half the time. So no refinement of the arcs can be checked against the lab yet.

The tuned Sr₂NiIrO₆ is the prettiest case: its arcs come out 68°, 110°, 71°, 110°, a clock with a half-turn symmetry, and that is the shape the published optimization lands on. The verdict: the quarter-turn picture is a faithful drawing of known chemistry, already in use under another name, and it gives no new prediction that data here can confirm.
