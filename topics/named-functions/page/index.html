The hypernatural logarithm lives on every conic, and its inverse is the hypernatural exponential

Brennan asked whether the hypernatural logarithm (formerly the supernatural logarithm) exists on shapes other than the hypercube, what its inverse should be called, and which other functions the theory keeps using deserve names.

Shapes. Yes on every conic of the plane dial except plain addition; on the simplex it is the same function's other half; on the ℓp balls it adds nothing new, because there the rung height is the p dial itself.

Inverse. Adopted (Brennan, Nov 26): the hypernatural exponential, exp⁽ˢ⁾(t) = 1 + Eˢ(t). "Natural exponential function" is a real textbook name for eˣ, and the closest published family already pairs hyperlogarithm with hyperexponential.

Candidates. Nine recurring combinations beyond the operations catalog: seven already have names to adopt, two need ours.

Which shapes carry it

Nothing in the construction needs the cube. A law needs one thing: a generator that touches the identity at the law's zero (G(x) = x + c·x² + … or x + c·x³ + …). Then G can be iterated any real number of times, the law gets a ladder of rungs, and G⁻ˢ is its hypernatural logarithm. The cube's case is G = eˣ − 1.

This fits the earlier polar-recast finding that no shape carries the cube's own function at fractional height: the rung-s law is not any conic's law. What is new is that every conic carries a ladder of its own. They are siblings, not one function.

What to call the inverse

  • "Natural exponential function" is real. Calculus textbooks use it for eˣ as the inverse of ln (Stewart's Calculus, section 6.3; the Whitman College calculus text). It is less common than "the exponential function", which is why it sounds odd.
  • The nearest family in print is the hyperlogarithm and hyperexponential "of strength γ" on the surreal numbers (Bagayoko and van der Hoeven, 2023). For whole strengths these are repeated logs and exps. Ours differ in two ways: real heights, and fused (our height-2 log is log(1 + log X), not log log X, so every curve keeps passing through (1, 0)). The word "natural" carries both.
  • The super-logarithm pairs differently: its inverse is tetration, the super-exponential. That is the "which rung am I on" direction, our Abel function, not ours.
  • Never shorten it. "Hyperlogarithm" also means Goncharov's iterated integrals for Feynman integrals, and "hyperexponential" means a probability distribution and a class of functions in differential algebra.
  • Notation (proposal, ours): height up top, dials below. Write ln⁽ˢ⁾ and exp⁽ˢ⁾ like +⁽ˢ⁾ and Eˢ. The subscript form lns collides with Tsallis's q-logarithm lnq, our closest cousin.

A lead worth following: Bell numbers at height s

The height counts something. The polynomials themselves are known (Comtet's triangle), and the whole-height counts are E. T. Bell's numbers of higher order. Reading them as the derivatives of the hypernatural exponential is ours. It is worth following because it gives height a counting meaning; the open question is what, if anything, a fractional height counts. Not checked yet.
